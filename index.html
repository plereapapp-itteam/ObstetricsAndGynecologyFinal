2. Labor Staging

Stage Begins Ends High-yield decision
Stage I True labor 10 cm Distinguish latent vs active; active phase ≈ 6 cm onward
Latent phase Labor onset ~6 cm Slow progress alone ≠ cesarean indication
Active phase ~6 cm 10 cm Assess Power–Passenger–Pelvis if progress slows
Stage II 10 cm Fetal delivery Assess descent/rotation; operative vaginal delivery vs CS
Stage III Fetal delivery Placental delivery Active management; placenta normally separates promptly; >30 min = retained

  • [SUPPLEMENTARY—CURRENT] Active-phase arrest: ≥6 cm, ruptured membranes, and no cervical change after 4 h of adequate uterine activity or 6 h of inadequate activity despite oxytocin.

  • Board distractor: 6 cm + 200 MVU + only 2 h with no change + reassuring fetus ≠ automatic cesarean.

  • The attached text itself notes that the historical 2-hour arrest rule may be too short and that waiting 4 or more hours allows many patients to deliver vaginally.

4. Fetal Heart-Rate Classification

Category Key finding Action
Category I Baseline 110–160 + moderate variability + no late/variable decels Continue surveillance
Category II Anything not I or III Correct reversible cause + close reassessment
Category III Absent variability + recurrent late/variable decels or bradycardia, OR sinusoidal pattern LIFE-SAVING: immediate resuscitative measures; expedite delivery if unresolved

  • Early deceleration → head compression.

  • Variable deceleration → cord compression.

  • Late deceleration → uteroplacental insufficiency.

  • Tachysystole + abnormal FHR → stop/reduce oxytocin, lateral position, correct hypotension; acute uterine relaxation may require terbutaline.

E. Placenta Accreta Spectrum — Board Rules

  • Previa + previous cesarean = PAS until excluded, especially an anterior placenta over the scar.

  • Hematuria + anterior previa + prior CS → percreta into bladder is a classic vignette.

  • Ultrasound clues: placental lacunae, thin myometrium, absent retroplacental clear zone, abnormal uterovesical vascularity.

  • FIRST-LINE operative strategy in extensive PAS: cesarean delivery without forcibly removing the placenta, leave placenta in situ, then planned hysterectomy.

  • ABSOLUTELY AVOID as a routine maneuver: forceful/manual placental extraction when PAS is strongly suspected because it can precipitate massive hemorrhage.

  • Preoperative preparation = blood products + anesthesia + experienced obstetric surgery ± urology/gynecologic oncology support.

F. Placental Abruption — Critical Lab / Delivery Rules

  • FIRST-LINE emergency approach: 2 large-bore IVs + CBC + type/cross + PT/PTT + fibrinogen + continuous fetal monitoring.

  • Fibrinogen <150 mg/dL in the source = clinically important hypofibrinogenemia/consumptive coagulopathy.

  • Massive blood loss may be concealed; maternal hypotension can appear late, so do not rely on visible blood volume alone.

  • LIFE-SAVING: maternal instability or persistent fetal compromise → resuscitation + prompt delivery.

  • Cesarean if fetus is viable, compromised, and vaginal delivery is not imminent.

  • Vaginal delivery preferred when fetus has died and maternal condition permits, because surgery in severe coagulopathy can worsen hemorrhage.

G. Vasa Previa — Must Not Miss

  • Risk factors: velamentous cord insertion, succenturiate/bilobed placenta, IVF, multiple gestation, previous low-lying placenta.

  • Best antepartum test: TVUS with color Doppler when risk factors are identified.

  • Bleeding + ROM + fetal bradycardia or sinusoidal tracing = fetal hemorrhage until proven otherwise.

  • Apt test: fetal blood remains pink after alkali exposure; maternal blood becomes yellow-brown.

  • Do not wait for Apt testing when fetus is compromised.

  • ABSOLUTELY AVOID: artificial rupture of membranes in known vasa previa.

  • LIFE-SAVING: emergency CS + neonatal team prepared for transfusion.

I. NATURAL FAMILY PLANNING, LACTATIONAL AMENORRHEA, AND BARRIER METHODS

Normal physiology or anatomy that the method targets

  • Normal ovulation follows coordinated hypothalamic GnRH → pituitary FSH/LH → follicular development → LH surge → ovulation, followed by corpus-luteum progesterone production.

  • Periodic abstinence attempts to identify the fertile window using menstrual timing, cervical mucus, basal body temperature, ovulation symptoms, or ovulation-prediction testing rather than suppressing ovulation.

  • Lactational amenorrhea relies on elevated prolactin suppressing pulsatile GnRH and therefore delaying ovulation after delivery.

  • Ovulation may return before the first postpartum menses, so amenorrhea alone outside strict lactational criteria does not reliably exclude fertility.

  • Barrier contraception prevents sperm from reaching the cervix/upper reproductive tract without altering ovarian function.

  • Male and internal condoms additionally reduce exposure to sexually transmitted pathogens, unlike hormonal methods and IUDs.

Risk factors, classification, and diagnostic/eligibility criteria

  • Natural methods: periodic abstinence, withdrawal/coitus interruptus, and lactational amenorrhea; the source characterizes these as the least reliable methods when pregnancy prevention is a high priority.

  • Periodic abstinence works best only when cycles are regular and the couple is highly motivated to identify the fertile window and abstain appropriately; source effectiveness is approximately 55%–80%.

  • Lactational amenorrhea method (LAM) requires all three practical source conditions: exclusive breastfeeding, continued amenorrhea, and ≤6 months postpartum.

  • Casual reliance on breastfeeding is unreliable: the source notes 50% of lactating mothers ovulate by 6–12 months and that ovulation may precede menses.

  • Male condom: source perfect-use efficacy about 98% and actual-use efficacy about 85%–90%.

  • Internal/female condom: source theoretical failure 5%, actual failure approximately 20%–25%; diaphragm + spermicide actual efficacy approximately 80%–85%.

  • Cervical cap: source actual efficacy approximately 68%–84%, with poorer efficacy in parous women.

Management by reproductive/postpartum stage

  • FIRST-LINE when STI protection is required: use a condom, because the source identifies condoms as the contraceptive method providing protection against HIV/STI transmission.

  • LAM: use only during the first 6 months postpartum, while amenorrheic and exclusively breastfeeding; switch/add contraception as soon as any criterion is lost.

  • Diaphragm: place with spermicide before intercourse, leave in place for at least 6 hours and no more than 30 hours after intercourse; additional intercourse within 6–8 hours requires more spermicide without removing the device.

  • The source recommends diaphragm replacement approximately every 2 years, refitting after pregnancy, and refitting after body-weight change of >20%.

  • Cervical cap: may be inserted up to 6 hours before intercourse and may remain in place 1–2 days in source teaching; refit after pregnancy or major weight change.

  • Spermicides alone are relatively weak contraception and do not protect against STIs; frequent nonoxynol-9 exposure may irritate vaginal mucosa and potentially increase susceptibility to infection in source teaching.

Maternal/fetal complications, prevention, and future-pregnancy counselling

  • Barrier and natural methods contain no systemic hormone and therefore do not cause fetal drug exposure if pregnancy occurs.

  • Pregnancy after method failure should be evaluated normally; no source-based indication exists for pregnancy termination merely because a barrier or natural method failed.

  • Diaphragm complications include UTI/bladder irritation, latex/spermicide hypersensitivity, and rare toxic shock syndrome if retained too long.

  • LAM has no adverse effect on breastfeeding and supports infant exposure to breast milk, but it should not be continued as sole contraception after the strict 6-month/amenorrhea/exclusive-feeding window.

  • No special interpregnancy interval is dictated by barrier-method use; spacing counseling should be based on maternal recovery and reproductive preference.

  • For couples for whom avoiding pregnancy is critical, move from user-dependent natural/barrier methods to LARC or permanent contraception.

II. INTRAUTERINE DEVICES — COPPER IUD AND LEVONORGESTREL IUD

Normal physiology or anatomy that the method targets

  • Normal fertilization requires viable sperm to traverse cervical mucus and the uterus to reach the fallopian tube.

  • Copper IUD produces a local sterile inflammatory response that impairs sperm function, motility, and fertilization; the source states IUDs do not suppress ovulation.

  • Levonorgestrel IUD (LNG-IUD) adds progestin-mediated cervical-mucus thickening and endometrial atrophy to the local inflammatory effect.

  • IUD strings extend through the cervix and allow detection of expulsion/migration and facilitate removal.

  • Copper IUD tends to increase menstrual bleeding/cramping, whereas LNG-IUD typically decreases bleeding and may cause amenorrhea.

  • The source explicitly describes IUDs as acting mainly through sperm/fertilization mechanisms and not as abortifacients.

Risk factors, classification, and diagnostic/eligibility criteria

  • Source absolute IUD contraindications: known/suspected pregnancy, undiagnosed abnormal bleeding, acute cervical/uterine/salpingeal infection, copper allergy/Wilson disease for Cu-IUD, and current breast cancer for LNG-IUD.

  • Source relative contraindications include prior ectopic pregnancy, STI in the preceding 3 months, uterine anomaly/fibroid distorting the cavity, and severe menorrhagia/dysmenorrhea for copper IUD.

  • Before insertion, the source vignette recommends pregnancy testing and gonorrhea/chlamydia testing when clinically appropriate.

  • First-year source failure rates are approximately 0.8% for ParaGard and 0.2% for Mirena; cumulative source rates are approximately 1.9% at 10 years for ParaGard and 0.7% at 5 years for Mirena.

  • Pelvic infection after insertion is primarily an insertion-related risk; the source states PID is rare beyond approximately 20 days after placement.

  • Uterine cavity distortion matters mechanically: significant cavity-distorting fibroids/anomalies reduce suitability because correct fundal placement cannot be assured.

Management by reproductive/postpartum stage

  • FIRST-LINE LARC: IUD is among the most effective reversible methods and minimizes user-adherence failure.

  • Copper IUD: preferred when a highly effective nonhormonal method is desired; source-era ParaGard duration is 10 years.

  • LNG-IUD: preferred when effective contraception plus reduction in heavy bleeding/dysmenorrhea is desirable; source reports amenorrhea in approximately 15%–20% of Mirena users.

  • Postpartum: immediate insertion is possible but carries a higher expulsion risk; Blueprints often places interval insertion at approximately the 6-week postpartum visit when follow-up is reliable.

  • Postabortion: Comprehensive Gynecology states an IUD may be inserted immediately when pregnancy has been ended and infection is not present.

  • Emergency contraception: copper IUD may be inserted within 120 hours / 5 days after unprotected intercourse and is the most effective EC method in the source.

Maternal/fetal complications, prevention, and future-pregnancy counselling

  • Complications include insertion pain, bleeding, expulsion, uterine perforation, insertion-related PID, and rare pregnancy despite the device.

  • If pregnancy occurs with an IUD, evaluate urgently for ectopic pregnancy and assess device location; the source lists both intrauterine and ectopic pregnancy as serious IUD complications.

  • Copper IUD may worsen dysmenorrhea/menorrhagia; LNG-IUD usually reduces flow and therefore may be advantageous in anemia/heavy menstrual bleeding.

  • Fertility generally returns after device removal because neither copper nor LNG-IUD permanently suppresses ovarian function.

  • A history of IUD use by itself does not require delay before attempting future pregnancy after removal.

  • Counsel users to seek review for severe pain, fever, abnormal discharge, pregnancy symptoms, or inability to locate strings when string checks have been taught.

III. COMBINED ESTROGEN-PROGESTIN CONTRACEPTION

Pills • Transdermal Patch • Vaginal Ring

Normal physiology or anatomy that the method targets

  • Normal ovulation requires pulsatile GnRH, FSH recruitment of a dominant follicle, and an LH surge.

  • Combined hormonal contraception (CHC) suppresses FSH and LH, preventing dominant-follicle development and the LH surge.

  • The progestin component also thickens cervical mucus and produces a thin/decidualized endometrium.

  • Withdrawal bleeding during the hormone-free interval is not a normal endogenous menstrual cycle; it occurs because exogenous steroid exposure stops.

  • Modern source OCP formulations usually contain approximately 20–35 µg ethinyl estradiol; high-dose formulations are described as approximately 50 µg.

  • Patch and ring use the same basic endocrine mechanism as combined pills.

Risk factors, classification, and diagnostic/eligibility criteria

  • Source actual-use OCP failure is approximately 8%, despite a theoretical first-year failure of <1%.

  • Source patch pregnancy rate is about 1%, with reduced efficacy in women weighing >198 lb / 90 kg.

  • Vaginal ring actual-use failure is approximately 1%–2%.

  • Source-era absolute CHC contraindications include thromboembolism/PE, CAD, stroke, smokers >35 years, breast/endometrial cancer, unexplained bleeding, abnormal liver function, known/suspected pregnancy, severe hypercholesterolemia, and severe hypertriglyceridemia.

  • Source specifically states that women >35 years who smoke ≥15 cigarettes/day should not use combined OCPs because of increased MI, stroke, DVT, and PE risk.

  • MEC threshold: CHC is category 4 with migraine with aura, systolic BP ≥160 or diastolic BP ≥100 mm Hg, vascular disease, hepatocellular adenoma, or malignant hepatoma in the source MEC sample.

Management by reproductive/postpartum stage

  • Appropriate candidate: healthy patient without estrogen-related thrombotic/vascular contraindications who can adhere to daily/weekly/monthly dosing.

  • Traditional pill schedule: source regimen commonly uses 21 active days + 7 hormone-free days; 24/4, extended-cycle, and continuous regimens are also described.

  • Patch: apply one patch weekly for 3 weeks, then 1 week off; source notes higher average estrogen exposure than a standard 35-µg pill.

  • Ring: keep in place for 3 weeks, remove for 1 week; if removed during intercourse in the source, replace within 3 hours.

  • Breastfeeding/postpartum: Blueprints advises avoiding estrogen while breastfeeding is being established because estrogen may decrease milk quantity/quality and early postpartum estrogen increases VTE risk.

  • Source postpartum teaching allows CHC approximately after 3 weeks in women without added VTE risk and later—around 6 weeks—when thrombotic risk or breastfeeding concerns persist; individual MEC classification overrides this general rule.

Maternal/fetal complications, prevention, and future-pregnancy counselling

  • Major adverse events are DVT, PE, stroke, MI, and hypertension, especially in smokers and patients with vascular risk.

  • Other source complications include gallbladder disease and rare benign hepatic tumors.

  • Common nonserious effects are nausea, headache, breakthrough bleeding, and perceived weight change.

  • Noncontraceptive benefits include reduced menstrual flow, anemia, dysmenorrhea, functional ovarian cysts, PID, endometrial cancer, and ovarian cancer.

  • If pregnancy occurs, stop CHC; contraceptive use itself does not constitute a reason for pregnancy termination in the retrieved source.

  • Fertility generally returns after stopping CHC; no disease-specific interpregnancy delay is required.

IV. PROGESTIN-ONLY CONTRACEPTION

POP • DMPA • Etonogestrel Implant • LNG-IUD Cross-Link

Normal physiology or anatomy that the method targets

  • Progestin-only methods primarily thicken cervical mucus, inhibit sperm transport, thin/atrophy the endometrium, and variably suppress ovulation.

  • Traditional source norethindrone POP 0.35 mg daily suppresses ovulation in only about 50% of cycles, so precise daily timing is critical.

  • DMPA more consistently suppresses ovulation and reduces tubal motility.

  • Etonogestrel implant suppresses ovulation and provides continuous progestin exposure without daily adherence.

  • Progestin-only methods avoid estrogen-mediated increases in coagulation and therefore suit many women in whom CHC is unacceptable.

  • Bleeding patterns often become irregular because endometrial proliferation is suppressed unevenly.

Risk factors, classification, and diagnostic/eligibility criteria

  • POP source failure is >8% with typical use, largely because late/missed pills reduce efficacy.

  • Source norethindrone POP should be taken at the same time daily; a delay of >3 hours is treated as a missed pill.

  • DMPA: source dose 150 mg IM every 3 months or 104 mg SC every 3 months; theoretical failure approximately 0.3%, typical-use failure about 3%.

  • DMPA suppresses ovulation for about 14 weeks, giving approximately a 2-week grace period beyond its usual 12-week schedule in source teaching.

  • Source Nexplanon failure ≈0.5% and provides approximately 3 years of contraception.

  • Progestin-only options are specifically favored in source examples for patients with migraine with aura, hypertension, smokers >35 years, CAD, or personal thromboembolism history when estrogen is inappropriate.

Management by reproductive/postpartum stage

  • POP: appropriate for breastfeeding and estrogen-contraindicated patients; emphasize same-time daily dosing.

  • DMPA: 150 mg IM every 3 months is source standard; counsel before first injection about irregular bleeding, weight change, delayed fertility return, and bone-density effects.

  • Implant: insert subdermally in the upper arm; no ongoing user action is required until removal/replacement.

  • Postpartum: POP, DMPA, implant, and LNG-IUD are source-favored choices for breastfeeding patients compared with estrogen-containing contraception.

  • DMPA bone health: encourage adequate calcium/vitamin D, smoking cessation, and weight-bearing exercise; the source states routine DXA monitoring is not indicated solely for DMPA use.

  • Enzyme-inducing antiepileptic drugs can reduce effectiveness of certain oral/implant progestins in the supporting gynecology source; method choice should therefore account for drug interactions.

Maternal/fetal complications, prevention, and future-pregnancy counselling

  • POP adverse effects include irregular bleeding, follicular cysts, acne, breast tenderness, and irritability.

  • DMPA causes irregular bleeding early; source reports amenorrhea in about 50% after 1 year and 80% after 5 years.

  • DMPA may cause reversible bone-mineral-density loss, greatest early in use, and recovery occurs over approximately 6 months–2 years after discontinuation in the source.

  • Source notes a potentially significant delay in return to fertility after DMPA, so avoid it when near-term conception is strongly desired.

  • Implant-associated irregular bleeding is the commonest reason for discontinuation; the source reports most bleeding is lighter than a normal period.

  • Pregnancy after implant/POP/DMPA failure should be managed as a normal early pregnancy unless another clinical problem is present; stop ongoing contraceptive exposure once pregnancy is confirmed.

V. EMERGENCY CONTRACEPTION

Normal physiology or anatomy that the method targets

  • Emergency contraception is used after unprotected intercourse but before an established pregnancy is recognized.

  • The source describes oral EC as delaying/suppressing ovulation and interfering with fertilization/tubal transport, while emphasizing that EC prevents pregnancy rather than terminating an implanted pregnancy.

  • Levonorgestrel EC primarily delays/inhibits ovulation and has fewer estrogen-related adverse effects than older combined regimens.

  • Ulipristal acetate is a selective progesterone-receptor modulator that delays follicular rupture; source dose is 30 mg once.

  • Copper IUD EC produces a local inflammatory environment hostile to sperm/fertilization.

  • EC does not provide ongoing protection from later intercourse in the same cycle unless a continuing method such as a copper IUD remains in place.

Risk factors, classification, and diagnostic/eligibility criteria

  • Indications include unprotected intercourse, condom breakage, missed pills, ring/patch displacement, IUD expulsion, or late DMPA injection.

  • Levonorgestrel: source preferred oral EC dose 1.5 mg PO once, ideally within 72 hours; some efficacy persists to 120 hours but decreases with delay.

  • Source oral EC failure when used within 72 hours is approximately 0.2%–3%.

  • Ulipristal: 30 mg PO once within 120 hours / 5 days.

  • Copper IUD: insert within 120 hours / 5 days; source estimates 99.8% efficacy, approximately 1 pregnancy per 1,000 users, making it the most effective EC option.

  • Oral EC is not intended for a known established pregnancy; the source notes no evidence of fetal harm if levonorgestrel-type EC is inadvertently taken during an existing pregnancy.

Management by stage

  • FIRST-LINE oral EC in the source: levonorgestrel 1.5 mg PO once, given as soon as possible.

  • Most effective EC overall: copper IUD within 5 days, if the patient is eligible for IUD insertion and desires ongoing contraception.

  • ALTERNATIVE oral EC: ulipristal 30 mg once within 120 hours; source suggests better efficacy than levonorgestrel during the later 72–120-hour interval.

  • Older combined estrogen-progestin EC regimens are less preferred because of more nausea/vomiting and lower efficacy than levonorgestrel-only regimens.

  • Pregnancy testing is required before copper-IUD EC placement and is recommended in the source before ulipristal.

  • EC should be followed by counseling on a reliable ongoing method because oral EC is a single-event intervention, not long-term contraception.

Maternal/fetal complications, prevention, and future-pregnancy counselling

  • Source oral EC adverse effects include nausea, vomiting, headache, dizziness, breast tenderness, and transient bleeding changes.

  • Source-era text labels ulipristal unsuitable in pregnancy and breastfeeding; this is preserved as source-era wording rather than generalized beyond the reference.

  • Copper-IUD EC shares the usual IUD risks: pain, heavier menses, perforation, expulsion, and infection around insertion.

  • Emergency levonorgestrel does not terminate an implanted pregnancy and is not an abortifacient in source teaching.

  • If the next menses is significantly delayed after EC, perform pregnancy testing rather than repeatedly dosing EC.

  • Future fertility is not impaired by oral EC or by copper-IUD removal.

VI. PERMANENT CONTRACEPTION — TUBAL STERILIZATION AND VASECTOMY

Normal physiology or anatomy that the method targets

  • Natural conception requires passage of sperm through the vas deferens and female reproductive tract, and passage of the ovum through a patent fallopian tube.

  • Tubal sterilization prevents sperm and ovum from meeting by occluding/dividing both fallopian tubes.

  • Vasectomy prevents sperm from entering the ejaculate by ligating/dividing the vas deferens.

  • Ovarian steroid production and menstrual hormonal cycles continue after tubal sterilization because the ovaries are not removed.

  • Testosterone production and sexual function remain intact after vasectomy because the testes remain hormonally functional.

  • Permanent contraception should be considered essentially irreversible during preprocedure counseling even though reversal techniques exist.

Risk factors, classification, and diagnostic/eligibility criteria

  • Appropriate candidates are patients/couples with a completed desired family size or for whom future pregnancy would pose unacceptable medical risk.

  • Source tubal-ligation failure is approximately 0.3%, though it varies with age, technique, and surgeon experience.

  • Source vasectomy actual failure rate is approximately 0.15%.

  • Pregnancy after tubal sterilization should prompt prompt evaluation for ectopic pregnancy because tubal failure carries ectopic risk.

  • Regret is highest among women who undergo sterilization before age 30, regardless of parity in the source.

  • Vasectomy is not immediately effective; semen analysis must confirm azoospermia, usually after approximately 6–8 weeks in the source.

Management by reproductive/postpartum stage

  • Postpartum tubal sterilization: may be performed through a small subumbilical incision using neuraxial anesthesia shortly after delivery when consent and logistics are appropriate.

  • Interval laparoscopic options in the source include bipolar fulguration, Falope rings, and clips; older hysteroscopic techniques described in the source are historical/source-era.

  • Counsel on LARC and vasectomy before female sterilization because similarly high efficacy can be achieved without permanent female surgery.

  • Vasectomy is typically an office procedure under local anesthesia and is generally simpler, safer, and less expensive than female sterilization.

  • Continue another contraceptive method after vasectomy until semen analysis demonstrates azoospermia.

  • Source postpartum discussion recommends that sterilization counseling begin during pregnancy, rather than waiting until immediately after delivery.

Maternal/fetal complications, prevention, and future-pregnancy counselling

  • Tubal sterilization operative risks include hemorrhage, infection, visceral/vascular injury, anesthesia complications, and conversion to laparotomy.

  • Source mortality from bilateral tubal ligation is approximately 4 per 100,000 women.

  • Vasectomy complications are usually minor—bleeding, infection, local anesthetic/suture reactions—and the source identifies no major long-term systemic effects.

  • If pregnancy occurs after sterilization, confirm location early because ectopic pregnancy must be excluded.

  • Reversal after tubal sterilization is technically possible but unreliable; source reversal success varies around 41%–84%, depending on method.

  • Patients uncertain about future childbearing should choose LARC rather than permanent sterilization.

XVI. POSTPARTUM / FAMILY-PLANNING ALGORITHM

  • Ask reproductive goal antenatally: spacing vs no further pregnancies.

  • Immediately postpartum → determine breastfeeding status + VTE risk + desire for LARC/permanent contraception.

  • Breastfeeding / early postpartum: favor progestin-only or LARC over estrogen in source teaching.

  • Wants highly effective reversible contraception → IUD or implant.

  • Wants nonhormonal highly effective contraception → copper IUD.

  • Wants permanent contraception → counsel tubal sterilization versus vasectomy, permanence, failure, regret, and surgical risk.

  • Wants STI prevention → condom regardless of additional contraceptive method.

  • Unsure about fertility plans → avoid permanent sterilization; choose reversible LARC.

XVII. METHOD-SELECTION ALGORITHM

  • Step 1: exclude pregnancy when relevant.

  • Step 2: ask whether STI protection is needed → condoms.

  • Step 3: screen for estrogen contraindications → VTE/PE, migraine aura, severe HTN, smoking >35, major vascular/liver disease.

  • Estrogen contraindicated → choose Cu-IUD, LNG-IUD, implant, DMPA, or appropriate POP according to MEC.

  • Heavy menses/anemia → favor LNG-IUD or hormonal suppression rather than copper IUD.

  • Wants lowest user error → choose IUD/implant.

  • Desires pregnancy soon → avoid methods with delayed return to fertility such as DMPA if that delay is unacceptable.

  • Completed family → offer permanent contraception only after thorough informed-consent counseling.

XVIII. EMERGENCY-CONTRACEPTION ALGORITHM

  • Unprotected intercourse / contraceptive failure → establish time since intercourse and whether pregnancy is already known.

  • ≤72 h: source oral first choice levonorgestrel 1.5 mg once.

  • 72–120 h: ulipristal 30 mg once is source-supported; copper IUD remains most effective.

  • ≤120 h and eligible for IUD: copper IUD → 99.8% source efficacy + continued long-term contraception.

  • Known established pregnancy → oral EC will not terminate it; copper-IUD EC is not inserted in a known pregnancy.

  • After oral EC → initiate/continue an ongoing contraceptive plan and advise pregnancy testing if expected menses is delayed.

XXIII. REQUIRED GRAPH / PATTERN DESCRIPTIONS

Hormonal profile across the menstrual cycle

  • Early follicular phase → FSH stimulates follicular recruitment.

  • Estradiol rises from the dominant follicle and suppresses FSH.

  • Midcycle estradiol feedback triggers the LH surge and ovulation.

  • Luteal corpus luteum produces progesterone; basal temperature rises.

  • CHC suppresses FSH/LH and removes the normal LH surge, preventing ovulation.

  • LAM suppresses hypothalamic pulsatile GnRH through prolactin and delays restoration of ovulation.

Normal and abnormal labor curve

  • Contraceptive choice has no role in diagnosing active labor.

  • Normal active labor begins around 6 cm cervical dilation in the broader obstetric framework.

  • Prior IUD/implant/OCP use does not alter labor-curve definitions once pregnancy occurs.

  • A contraceptive failure pregnancy is managed according to ordinary obstetric labor criteria.

  • Permanent contraception may be performed postpartum after delivery when previously chosen.

  • Labor abnormalities are managed independently of the contraceptive method planned postpartum.

Partogram

  • Cervical dilation/descent, contractions, fetal heart rate, membrane status, and maternal observations are plotted during labor.

  • The planned postpartum contraceptive method does not change partogram interpretation.

  • Counseling for postpartum contraception can occur antenatally and does not require waiting for the 6-week visit.

  • Postpartum sterilization planning should be completed before labor when possible.

  • IUD insertion immediately postpartum has greater expulsion risk than interval placement.

  • Fetal compromise always takes precedence over contraceptive procedure timing.

FHR patterns

  • Early deceleration → head compression.

  • Variable deceleration → cord compression.

  • Late deceleration → uteroplacental insufficiency.

  • Contraceptive history does not produce a characteristic FHR pattern.

  • Persistent Category III tracing requires obstetric intervention regardless of postpartum contraceptive plans.

  • Postpartum contraception is addressed only after maternal/fetal stabilization.
