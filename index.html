Labor and Parturition 5%

Obstetrics and Gynecology Board Review

Almost every Labor and Parturition item follows the same five-step sequence: read the cervical/stage data, read the fetal tracing, ask whether Power, Passenger, or Pelvis is the problem, pick an intervention, then decide route and timing of delivery. Anchor the numeric cutoffs (6 cm = active phase, >200 MVU, the Category III criteria, the 34/37/39-week delivery ladder) and the emergency drug pairings -- what's first-line, what's contraindicated -- and most vignettes become plug-and-chug.

🧭Core Frameworks

The Labor Curve & Active-Phase Arrest Rule
Latent (onset → ~6 cm)  →  Active (~6 cm → 10 cm)  →  Stage II (10 cm → birth)  →  Stage III (birth → placenta, normally <30 min)
arrest = ≥6 cm + ruptured membranes + NO change after 4h adequate UA or 6h inadequate UA despite oxytocin
The single most tested trap here is calling something 'arrest' too early. The old rule allowed only 2 hours of no change before diagnosing arrest -- current teaching recognizes that's often too short, so a patient at ≥6 cm with ruptured membranes needs a full 4 hours of adequate contractions (>200 Montevideo units/10 min) -- or 6 hours of inadequate contractions despite oxytocin -- without cervical change before active-phase arrest, and therefore cesarean, is diagnosed. Slow progress in the latent phase alone is never, by itself, an indication for cesarean.
Bishop Score Sets the Induction Strategy
Score >8 = favorable (induce directly)  |  Score ≤5 = unfavorable (ripen cervix first)  |  components: Dilatation, Effacement, Station, Consistency, Position
a high score predicts induction success close to spontaneous labor; a low score predicts oxytocin alone will fail
The Bishop score is shorthand for how much the cervix has already done labor's work on its own. Dilatation, effacement, and station each carry 0-3 points; consistency and position max out at 2. A score above 8 means the cervix is essentially labor-ready, so oxytocin alone will likely succeed. A score of 5 or below predicts a high failure rate for oxytocin alone, so a cervical ripening agent (mechanical or prostaglandin) should come first.
Fetal Heart Rate: Three Categories, One Life-Saving One
Category I: 110-160 bpm + moderate variability + no late/variable decels → routine surveillance  |  Category II: everything else → correct reversible cause + close reassessment  |  Category III: absent variability + recurrent late/variable decels or bradycardia, OR sinusoidal → resuscitate now, expedite delivery if unresolved
early decel = head compression  |  variable decel = cord compression  |  late decel = uteroplacental insufficiency
Category II is a huge, heterogeneous bucket -- it is NOT an automatic cesarean, it's a prompt to look for and fix a reversible cause (stop oxytocin, reposition, correct hypotension, consider amnioinfusion or terbutaline for tachysystole/hypertonus). Category III is the only category that is immediately life-threatening and demands intrauterine resuscitation with expedited delivery if it doesn't resolve. Knowing which deceleration pattern maps to which mechanism lets you predict the fix before you're told the answer.
The Obstetric Emergency Drug Ladder
Eclampsia → MgSO₄ 6 g IV load/15-20min then 2 g/h  |  Severe HTN → IV labetalol or hydralazine (oral nifedipine also accepted)  |  Atony/PPH → massage + oxytocin → methylergonovine/carboprost/misoprostol → TXA within 3h → balloon → surgery
know the contraindication PAIRING, not just the drug: methylergonovine=HTN/preeclampsia, carboprost=asthma, MgSO₄=myasthenia gravis
Boards love pairing an emergency drug with the one comorbidity that makes it dangerous. Methylergonovine causes vasoconstriction, so it's out in hypertension/preeclampsia. Carboprost is a prostaglandin that can bronchoconstrict, so it's out in asthma. Magnesium sulfate worsens neuromuscular weakness, so it's used cautiously in myasthenia gravis. For PPH specifically, current WHO teaching adds early tranexamic acid (TXA) 1 g IV within 3 hours of birth alongside uterotonics -- but TXA itself is avoided when there is a clear thromboembolic contraindication.

Prenatal Care, Screening & Physiologic Changes 5%

Obstetrics and Gynecology Board Review

Almost every question in this topic tests one of three moves: dating the pregnancy correctly, picking the right screening test for the right week (and knowing that a positive screen is never the diagnosis), or recognizing that a lab value that would be abnormal outside pregnancy is expected inside it. Anchor the timeline of visits and the screen-versus-diagnostic split, and the numeric cutoffs stop being a memorization slog and start being predictable.

🧭Core Frameworks

Confirm → Date → Screen: The Master Sequence
Confirm pregnancy → establish dates (LMP/Nägele or ultrasound) → baseline labs → aneuploidy/structural screening → metabolic screening (GDM) → growth surveillance → delivery planning
every prenatal visit is really just asking 'where are we in this sequence?'
The source explicitly frames prenatal care as this priority chain, and it is the skeleton every vignette hangs on. A stem that gives you a positive pregnancy test with an uncertain LMP is testing step 2 (get a dating ultrasound); a stem at 26 weeks with a normal anatomy scan is testing step 5 (GDM screen). Before reaching for a specific number, first ask which link in this chain the vignette is standing on — it narrows the answer choices immediately.
Dating Hierarchy: Whoever Is Earliest and Most Certain Wins
Certain LMP + regular cycles → Nägele's rule (LMP − 3 mo + 7 d + 1 yr)  |  Uncertain LMP or size/date mismatch → ultrasound  |  Once dates are set, a later small scan does NOT redate the pregnancy
the earliest reliable estimate becomes the permanent EDD
Ultrasound dating is most accurate in the first trimester and gets progressively less precise as pregnancy advances, so the source's rule is to lock in dates as early as possible and then defend them. A classic trap is a fetus that measures small on a third-trimester scan after dates were confirmed early — the correct move is to evaluate for growth restriction, not to shift the EDD backward to make the fetus look 'on track.'
Screening Is Not Diagnosis
cfDNA, NT + PAPP-A/β-hCG, quad screen, MSAFP, anatomy ultrasound = SCREENING  →  CVS (9–12 wk), amniocentesis (>15 wk), PUBS = DIAGNOSTIC
a positive screen buys you a diagnostic test, never a treatment plan
Every screening test in this topic estimates risk; none of them confirms a chromosomal or structural diagnosis. The board pattern is a positive or high-risk screen (elevated MSAFP, a high-risk cfDNA result) followed by a question asking for the next step — the correct answer is almost always a diagnostic test (CVS or amniocentesis depending on gestational age) or, for an isolated abnormal MSAFP, first verifying dates and looking for multiple gestation before jumping to invasive testing.
GDM: The Two-Step Cascade
24–28 wk: 50-g 1-h GLT → if ≥140 mg/dL, reflex to 100-g 3-h OGTT (fasting/1h/2h/3h cutoffs 95/180/155/140) → GDM diagnosed if ≥2 values are abnormal
one abnormal value on the OGTT is not enough — you need two
The 50-g test is a screen with a single cutoff; the 100-g test is the diagnostic step and requires meeting or exceeding at least two of the four thresholds. A board trap sets up a patient with exactly one elevated OGTT value and asks whether she has GDM — she does not, though she still warrants closer surveillance and often a repeat test, since a single abnormality is not diagnostic on its own.

Hemorrhagic Complications of Pregnancy 5%

Obstetrics and Gynecology Board Review

Every hemorrhage vignette in this topic is really asking the same three questions: is this painless or painful, is the uterus soft or hypertonic, and did it happen right after the membranes ruptured? Painless bright-red bleeding means previa (and previa plus a prior cesarean means accreta spectrum until proven otherwise); painful bleeding with a firm, tender uterus means abruption; sudden pain with fetal bradycardia in a scarred uterus means rupture; and bleeding right after ROM with a sinusoidal tracing means vasa previa. Sort the stem into one of these four buckets and the 'next step' answer almost always follows automatically.

🧭Core Frameworks

The Bleeding-Pattern Master Key
Painless, bright red → PREVIA  |  Painful + firm/tender uterus → ABRUPTION  |  Scarred uterus + sudden pain + bradycardia + ↓station → RUPTURE  |  Bleeding after ROM + sinusoidal/bradycardia → VASA PREVIA
pain and uterine tone diagnose faster than any ultrasound
Third-trimester bleeding vignettes are built to be sorted by two bedside features: whether there is pain, and what the uterus feels like. Painless bleeding with a soft, non-tender uterus is previa — stabilize and get a transvaginal ultrasound, and never perform a digital vaginal exam until previa is excluded. Painful bleeding with a firm, tender, hypertonic uterus is abruption, which remains a clinical diagnosis; a negative ultrasound does not rule it out. A scarred uterus with sudden pain, fetal bradycardia, and loss of station is rupture — a race-to-the-OR emergency. Bleeding that starts right after rupture of membranes, paired with a sinusoidal or bradycardic tracing, is vasa previa until proven otherwise.
Placenta Accreta Spectrum: Previa + Prior Cesarean = PAS Until Excluded
Previa + 1/2/3/4/5 prior CS → accreta risk ~3% / 11% / 40% / 61% / 67%  |  Accreta (adherent) → Increta (into myometrium) → Percreta (through serosa ± bladder/rectum)
leave the placenta in situ — do not pull it
Every additional prior cesarean scar under an anterior previa multiplies the chance of abnormal placental adherence, which is why 'previa + prior CS' should trigger a dedicated PAS workup (ultrasound for lacunae, thin myometrium, absent retroplacental clear zone, abnormal uterovesical vessels, ± MRI if depth is equivocal). Anterior previa + prior CS + new hematuria is the classic percreta-into-bladder vignette. When PAS is confirmed or strongly suspected, the operative rule overrides instinct: deliver the baby by cesarean, leave the placenta in situ rather than forcing separation, and proceed to a planned hysterectomy (~34 weeks) with blood products and experienced surgical support ready — forceful extraction can trigger 3,000–5,000 mL of sudden blood loss.
PPH: 4 Ts and the Escalation Ladder
Tone → Trauma → Tissue → Thrombin  |  Massage + oxytocin → 2nd-line uterotonic (per contraindications) → Bakri balloon / embolization → laparotomy ± vessel ligation → hysterectomy
match the next uterotonic to what the patient can't tolerate
PPH (>500 mL after vaginal birth, >1,000 mL after cesarean; early if <24 h, delayed if >24 h) is worked up by the 4 Ts while treatment escalates in parallel. Atony (Tone) is by far the most common cause, so massage plus oxytocin is always first-line. If bleeding persists, the next uterotonic is chosen by what the patient cannot tolerate — methylergonovine is avoided in hypertension/preeclampsia, carboprost is avoided in asthma. Bleeding despite drugs escalates to a Bakri balloon or uterine artery embolization, and refractory hemorrhage escalates further to laparotomy with vascular ligation and, if uncontrolled, hysterectomy. Bleeding that resists uterotonics alongside a placenta that will not separate should make you suspect PAS instead of simple atony.
Vasa Previa: the Fetal-Exsanguination Emergency
Risk factors (velamentous cord, succenturiate/bilobed placenta, IVF, multiples, prior low-lying placenta) → TVUS + color Doppler → planned CS ~35 wk  |  ROM + bleeding + sinusoidal/bradycardia → emergency CS now
fetal blood volume is only ~80–100 mL/kg — small losses are catastrophic
Vasa previa (~1:2,500) is dangerous because the bleeding is fetal, not maternal — a term fetus carries only roughly 80–100 mL/kg of blood, so what looks like modest bleeding can be lethal (perinatal mortality ~40–60% after rupture). Patients with recognized risk factors get targeted color Doppler screening and a planned cesarean around 35 weeks, before labor can rupture the membranes over the vessels. If bleeding occurs after ROM with a sinusoidal or bradycardic tracing, treat it as fetal hemorrhage until proven otherwise and proceed to emergency cesarean — do not wait for a confirmatory Apt test (fetal blood stays pink after alkali exposure; maternal blood turns yellow-brown) if the fetus is compromised. Artificial rupture of membranes is absolutely avoided whenever vasa previa is known or suspected.

Early Pregnancy Risk, Bleeding & Gestational Trophoblastic Disease 6%

Obstetrics and Gynecology Board Review

Nearly every question in this topic opens the same way: a bleeding, hCG-positive patient, and the very first branch point is stability, not gestational age or a specific number. An unstable patient goes straight to the OR; a stable one gets trended over time. From there, each subtopic repeats the same three-step pattern: a numeric threshold defines the category (discriminatory zone, hCG greater than 100,000, WHO score of 7 or higher), the category dictates the first-line action (serial hCG, suction D&C, single-agent chemotherapy), and a short list of contraindication pairings tells you which drug to avoid. Learn that pattern once and ectopic pregnancy, PPH, and molar disease/GTN stop feeling like three separate topics.

🧭Core Frameworks

The Instability Override
Unstable / hemorrhaging / eclamptic / infected / abrupting / nonreassuring fetus  >>>  any gestational-age-based algorithm
stabilize and act now beats waiting for the usual numeric cutoff
Every algorithm in this topic — PUL work-up, preeclampsia delivery timing, PPROM management, tocolysis — assumes a stable mother and fetus. The instant that assumption breaks (peritoneal signs, uncontrolled hemorrhage, an active seizure, chorioamnionitis, abruption, or a nonreassuring tracing), the routine cutoff stops applying and the answer becomes immediate stabilization plus delivery or surgery, regardless of gestational age. Boards test this by dangling a tempting numeric rule ("deliver at 34 weeks," "tocolyse until steroids finish") in a vignette where the patient is actually unstable — the override, not the number, is the correct answer.
The Ectopic / PUL Ladder
Discriminatory zone (~1,500-2,000 mIU/mL) → no IUP = PUL → serial β-hCG q48h → MTX if hCG <5,000 + mass <5cm + no cardiac activity → day 4→7 decline ≥15%
each rung has its own number, and the number tells you the next move
A stable patient with a hCG at or above the discriminatory zone but no visible intrauterine pregnancy is a pregnancy of unknown location (PUL), not an automatic ectopic diagnosis — the next step is trending, not treating. Once an ectopic is confirmed, three numbers decide whether methotrexate is reasonable: hCG under 5,000, mass generally under 5 cm, and no fetal cardiac activity. After treatment, the day 4 to day 7 hCG decline is the single checkpoint — a fall of 15% or more means continue weekly monitoring to zero; anything less means retreat or move to surgery.
PPH: the 4 T's Meet the Drug Ladder
Tone → oxytocin (avoid in HTN? no — methylergonovine is)  |  Trauma → repair  |  Tissue → D&C  |  Thrombin → correct coagulopathy
a boggy uterus is atony; a firm uterus with brisk bleeding is trauma
Postpartum hemorrhage management starts with resuscitation while you sort the cause into Tone (atony — the most common cause, treated with uterotonics and massage), Trauma (lacerations — suspected when the uterus is firm but bleeding continues), Tissue (retained products — treated with D&C), or Thrombin (coagulopathy). Oxytocin is first-line for atony; if it fails, the two classic contraindication pairings are methylergonovine avoided in hypertension/preeclampsia, and carboprost avoided/used with caution in asthma. Refractory, life-threatening bleeding escalates to Bakri balloon or uterine artery embolization, then laparotomy, then hysterectomy as the definitive step.
GTD Staircase: Mole → Surveillance → GTN → Stage:Score → Treat
Evacuate mole → hCG should fall → plateau/rise = GTN → FIGO stage (I-IV) : WHO score (0-6 vs ≥7) → single-agent vs multiagent chemo
reported together as one stage:score pair, e.g. III:5
After suction D&C for a mole, hCG should steadily decline; if it instead plateaus (4 values over at least 3 weeks) or rises (3 values over at least 2 weeks), that alone is diagnostic of postmolar gestational trophoblastic neoplasia — no repeat tissue sampling is required. Every case of GTN then gets a FIGO anatomic stage (I confined to the uterus, II beyond the uterus but within the genital tract, III lung metastases, IV other distant sites, especially brain or liver) plus a WHO prognostic score built from age, antecedent pregnancy, interval, hCG level, tumor size, metastatic site, and number of metastases. A summed score of 0-6 is low-risk and gets single-agent chemotherapy; 7 or higher is high-risk and needs multiagent EMA/CO.

Fetal Complications of Pregnancy 5%

Obstetrics and Gynecology Board Review

Almost every vignette in this topic runs the same four-step skeleton: confirm the dates, name the abnormality (too big, too small, or lying wrong), check whether the fetus is actually tolerating things, then decide surveillance versus delivery. Once that skeleton is automatic, the specific cutoffs — 10th/90th percentile, 4,500 g, absent diastolic flow — just slot into steps two and three.

🧭Core Frameworks

The Fetal-Complications Decision Sequence
Confirm dating  →  Define presentation/growth abnormality  →  Assess fetal reserve (NST/BPP/Doppler)  →  Surveillance OR delivery
the master algorithm every vignette in this topic is built on
Nearly every question here is really asking you where in this chain the scenario sits. Bad dating makes 'small' or 'big' meaningless, so it is always fixed first. Once the abnormality is named (SGA, LGA, macrosomia, breech, transverse), the next question is always whether the fetus currently looks reassuring — and only a nonreassuring fetus, not the diagnosis label itself, is what actually forces delivery.
Growth Abnormality Map
SGA <10th %ile (<5th/<3rd = higher risk)  |  LGA >90th %ile  |  Macrosomia >4,500 g  |  Symmetric FGR = early, global  vs  Asymmetric FGR = late, head-sparing
percentile labels the size; symmetry labels the timing of the insult
SGA/LGA are ultrasound-percentile labels assigned prenatally; macrosomia is a birth-weight threshold used mainly for delivery-route planning. Within FGR, symmetric growth restriction (head, abdomen, and long bones all proportionately small) usually points to an early insult — often before 20 weeks, such as aneuploidy or early infection — while asymmetric growth restriction (a relatively spared head with a small abdomen) points to a later uteroplacental insufficiency redirecting blood flow toward the brain.
Malpresentation Decision Tree
Frank/complete breech  →  ECV 37–38 wk  →  failed repeat ~39 wk  →  CS commonly  |  Footling/transverse  →  CS  |  Face: mentum anterior = vaginal OK, mentum posterior = CS unless it rotates
the shape of the presenting part decides whether a vaginal trial is even on the table
Frank and complete breech (hips flexed) keep the door open for ECV or, if that fails and strict criteria are met, a selected vaginal trial with an experienced provider. Footling/incomplete breech and persistent transverse lie close that door — cesarean is the default. For face presentation, everything hinges on the chin: mentum anterior can deliver vaginally, but persistent mentum posterior or transverse needs either rotation or cesarean, because a posterior chin cannot navigate the pelvis by extension the way an anterior chin can.
Shoulder Dystocia: the Timed Maneuver Ladder
Call for help + stop pushing + time it  →  McRoberts  →  suprapubic pressure  →  Rubin / Woods corkscrew  →  deliver posterior arm  →  Zavanelli + emergent CS (rescue)
escalate in order, target <5 minutes, NEVER fundal pressure
Shoulder dystocia is an unpredictable intrapartum emergency — many cases occur below classic macrosomic weight thresholds, so you cannot rely on 'the baby wasn't that big' to rule it out. The maneuvers all share one mechanical goal: free or rotate the impacted anterior shoulder from behind the pubic symphysis. Fundal pressure pushes the shoulder harder into the symphysis and is never used. If every maneuver fails, Zavanelli (cephalic replacement) with emergency cesarean is the rescue, and symphysiotomy is an extreme last resort.

Hypertension During Pregnancy 4%

Obstetrics and Gynecology Board Review

Almost every hypertension-in-pregnancy vignette is the same five-step pipeline: cutoff → classify → severe feature? → treat → deliver. Once you own the three numbers that gate every decision (140/90, proteinuria ≥300 mg/24h or P/C ≥0.3, and severe BP ≥160/110) plus the four-drug eclampsia card, most 'next best step' questions answer themselves. The traps are almost always about sequencing — treating before delivering, chasing a confirmatory lab before treating an active seizure, or reaching for the one contraindicated uterotonic.

🧭Core Frameworks

The Cutoff Ladder: HTN → Proteinuria → Severe
New HTN after 20 wk = ≥140/90 mm Hg ×2, ≥4 h apart  →  Proteinuria = ≥300 mg/24 h or P/C ratio ≥0.3  →  Severe BP = ≥160 systolic and/or ≥110 diastolic
three numbers, three separate decisions
The first number simply tells you whether the patient is hypertensive at all after 20 weeks. The second decides whether she crosses into preeclampsia territory. The third is the emergency trigger that demands urgent treatment within minutes, not the usual 4-hour repeat confirmation. Dipstick +1 is only acceptable when quantitative testing (24-h collection or P/C ratio) is unavailable — a dipstick alone is a fallback, never the preferred confirmatory test.
Classify Before You Treat: The Six-Way Split
Gestational HTN → Preeclampsia (± severe features) → Eclampsia  |  Chronic HTN → Superimposed PE  |  HELLP / AFLP as parallel severe syndromes
timing relative to 20 weeks (and postpartum) is what separates the two trees
Gestational HTN and preeclampsia both start fresh after 20 weeks. Chronic HTN, by contrast, is defined by timing outside that window — present before conception, before 20 weeks, or still present more than 6 weeks postpartum. Superimposed preeclampsia is chronic HTN plus new or worsening proteinuria or end-organ injury layered on top. HELLP and AFLP are severe syndromes that can complicate either branch and sometimes present with minimal or absent hypertension/proteinuria themselves — do not wait for classic numbers before suspecting them in the right clinical picture (RUQ pain, hemolysis, low platelets, or hypoglycemia with hepatic failure).
The Eclampsia Life-Saving Sequence
ABCs → MgSO4 6 g IV over 15–20 min → 2 g/h maintenance → treat severe BP (labetalol / hydralazine / nifedipine) → delivery only after stabilization
delivery is definitive therapy, never step one of an active seizure
A recurrent seizure gets an additional 2 g MgSO4 IV over 3–5 minutes. Magnesium toxicity is a clinical ladder — deep tendon reflex loss around 10 mg/mL, respiratory depression around 15 mg/mL, cardiac arrest at 20–25 mg/mL — and the antidote is 10 mL of 10% calcium gluconate/chloride IV. Two classic board traps live here: phenytoin is not first-line routine eclampsia therapy, and cesarean delivery is never the first move on an actively seizing patient — stabilize the mother first.
Delivery Timing Runs on Severity, Not One Clock
GHTN & PE without severe features → 37 wk  |  PE with severe features → 34 wk if stable  |  HELLP ≥34 wk → now; <34 wk stable → steroids → deliver in 24–48 h  |  Eclampsia / AFLP / deterioration → deliver now, any GA
severity shortens the clock; instability erases it entirely
Think of it as a shrinking window: the mildest disease is managed to near-term (37 wk), severe features pull that window in to 34 wk (with expectant inpatient management permitted below 34 wk only while mother and fetus stay stable), and true emergencies — eclampsia, AFLP, uncontrolled severe HTN, DIC, pulmonary edema, abruption, or persistent fetal compromise — override gestational age altogether and demand delivery as soon as the mother is stabilized.

Diabetes During Pregnancy 4%

Obstetrics and Gynecology Board Review

Every diabetes-in-pregnancy vignette is really testing the same relay race: a screening number sends you to a diagnostic test, the diagnostic test sorts the patient into a treatment class, the treatment class sets a surveillance schedule, and the surveillance/control picture sets delivery timing. Memorize the numbers at each handoff -- 140, 95/180/155/140, 90/140/120, 4,500 g -- and you can solve almost any stem in this topic without needing to recall a single paragraph of prose.

🧭Core Frameworks

The Screen → Diagnose Ladder
24–28 wk universal screen (first visit if high-risk, repeat 24–28 wk if negative) → 50-g 1-h GLT, positive if >140 mg/dL → 100-g 3-h GTT: 95 / 180 / 155 / 140 mg/dL → ≥2 abnormal values = GDM
each rung is a screening test for the next rung, not a diagnosis by itself
The 50-g glucose loading test is only ever a screen -- a value above 140 mg/dL does not diagnose GDM, it earns the patient a confirmatory 100-g, 3-hour oral glucose tolerance test. On that test, the four cutoffs (fasting 95, 1-hour 180, 2-hour 155, 3-hour 140 mg/dL) are compared against each drawn value, and the diagnosis of GDM requires at least two abnormal values, not one. Patients who are high-risk at baseline (obesity, prior GDM, strong family history, prior macrosomic infant) are screened at the very first prenatal visit and, if that is negative, screened again at the routine 24-28 week window.
White Classification: A1/A2 vs. the Vascular Letters
A1 = GDM, diet/exercise-controlled  |  A2 = GDM, medication-required  |  B/C/D = pregestational, sorted only by onset age & duration  |  F/R/RF/H/T = nephropathy / retinopathy / both / heart disease / transplant
end-organ vascular disease (F/R/RF/H/T) outpredicts the duration-based B/C/D letters
A1 and A2 are both gestational diabetes -- the only difference is whether diet and exercise alone keep glucose at goal (A1) or medication is required (A2), and A2 automatically earns intensified fetal surveillance. B, C, and D describe pregestational diabetes purely by age of onset and years of disease (for example, D = onset before age 10 or duration over 20 years) and say nothing about vascular health. F (nephropathy), R (proliferative retinopathy), RF (both), H (ischemic heart disease), and T (prior renal transplant) instead describe actual end-organ vascular damage -- and that vascular burden predicts a worse pregnancy outcome more reliably than how long the patient has simply had diabetes.
The Treatment Escalation Ladder
Diet + exercise + 4x/day glucose monitoring (fasting + 3 postprandial) → >25–30% of values elevated → insulin (first-line; individualized lispro/aspart + NPH pattern) → metformin (alternative if insulin refused) → glyburide (NOT first-line)
there is no single universal starting insulin dose -- titration is pattern-based
First-line management for every GDM patient is nonpharmacologic: a diabetic diet, exercise (a brisk ~15-minute walk starting 30-40 minutes after eating is specifically favored), and four-times-daily glucose checks. Medication is added once more than about 25-30% of those checked values run high, and insulin is the first-line drug -- individualized, not a fixed starting dose. Metformin is an accepted alternative for a patient who will not take insulin, understanding that it crosses the placenta and has more limited long-term outcome data. Glyburide is deliberately not first-line, because short-term outcomes were worse than with insulin -- it is not absolutely contraindicated, just outranked by the other two options.
Surveillance & Delivery-Timing Rule
A2 GDM: NST/modified BPP begin 32–36 wk (weekly-to-twice-weekly) + EFW US 34–37 wk → well controlled = 39 wk, poor control = 37–39 wk  |  Pregestational DM: testing begins ~32 wk (earlier if poorly controlled) → well controlled/uncomplicated = 39 wk  |  EFW >4,500 g = offer planned cesarean
diabetes alone is never, by itself, an indication for cesarean delivery
Once a patient needs medication (A2) or already has pregestational disease, antenatal testing starts well before term -- 32 to 36 weeks for A2 GDM, around 32 weeks (or earlier) for pregestational disease -- because these are the patients whose placental function can decline unpredictably. Delivery timing then follows control: well-controlled A2 or uncomplicated pregestational disease targets 39 weeks, while poor control, nonreassuring testing, worsening hypertension/renal disease, or growth restriction all push delivery earlier. The one delivery-route rule that stands alone is macrosomia: an estimated fetal weight over 4,500 g in a diabetic patient is when planned cesarean should specifically be offered -- diabetes by itself, at any control level, does not mandate one.

Seizures & Medical Illnesses During Pregnancy 4%

Obstetrics and Gynecology Board Review

This topic reads like ten unrelated mini-diseases stitched together, but almost every question tests the same three moves: sort a new seizure or a hypertensive picture using a hard numeric cutoff, match the right emergency drug to the right emergency (magnesium for eclampsia, never an antihypertensive alone), and know whether a risky drug is absolutely forbidden, merely avoided when a safer option exists, or contraindicated only in one specific comorbidity. Anchor those three moves and the rest of the medical-illness list — renal, thyroid, lupus, cardiac — becomes a matter of matching each disease to its own numeric ladder.

🧭Core Frameworks

New Seizure ≥20 wk: Eclampsia Until Proven Otherwise
Seizure + HTN/proteinuria/severe features after 20 wk  →  treat as ECLAMPSIA  →  MgSO₄ 6 g IV over 15–20 min → 2 g/h infusion; recurrent seizure → +2 g IV over 3–5 min
magnesium is not an antihypertensive — treat the blood pressure separately
The single biggest trap in this topic is reflexively reaching for a routine antiepileptic (phenytoin, a benzodiazepine) the moment a pregnant patient seizes. If she is ≥20 weeks and has hypertension, proteinuria, or another severe feature, the source is explicit: treat as eclampsia first and give magnesium sulfate, not a routine AED. A known epileptic who seizes without new preeclampsia features instead gets her home anticonvulsant continued or restored — the deciding question is always 'does this look like preeclampsia,' not 'does she have a seizure history.'
Renal Disease Severity Ladder
Mild: Cr <1.5 mg/dL  →  Moderate: Cr 1.5–2.8 mg/dL  →  Severe: Cr >2.8 mg/dL
the same three cutoffs recur across the chapter as the renal-risk yardstick
Chronic kidney disease in pregnancy is graded almost entirely by one number. Mild disease usually tolerates pregnancy reasonably well but still raises preeclampsia and fetal-growth-restriction risk; moderate disease carries a meaningfully higher chance of pregnancy-related renal decline; severe disease (Cr >2.8) carries a high risk of persistent deterioration or dialysis. Memorize this ladder once and it pays off twice — for direct CKD-staging questions, and because the same creatinine number reappears as a severe-feature criterion for preeclampsia (Cr >1.1 or doubling from baseline).
Lupus Flare vs Preeclampsia — Same Symptoms, Different Mechanism
Low complement (C3/C4) + active urine sediment  →  lupus flare  |  Normal complement + inactive sediment  →  preeclampsia
both can show hypertension, proteinuria, and thrombocytopenia — complement and sediment break the tie
SLE and preeclampsia overlap almost completely on the surface — either can produce rising blood pressure, worsening proteinuria, and a falling platelet count in the third trimester. The distinguishing pair the source relies on is complement level and urine sediment activity: a lupus flare consumes complement (low C3/C4) and produces an active sediment, while preeclampsia typically leaves complement normal and the sediment inactive. Get the diagnosis right because the treatments diverge completely — immunosuppression for a flare versus obstetric stabilization and possible delivery for preeclampsia.
Three Tiers of Drug Danger in Pregnancy
Absolutely contraindicated (methotrexate, isotretinoin)  →  Avoid when a safer alternative exists (valproate, carbamazepine, ACE-I/ARB, warfarin)  →  Contraindicated only in one comorbidity (methylergonovine + HTN, carboprost + asthma)
not every 'avoid in pregnancy' drug is an absolute contraindication — read which tier the question is testing
Board questions punish sloppy labeling of every risky drug as 'absolutely contraindicated.' Methotrexate and isotretinoin truly are — never given in an ongoing viable pregnancy. Valproate, carbamazepine, ACE inhibitors/ARBs, and warfarin sit one tier down: avoided whenever an effective safer option exists, but not universally forbidden (warfarin has a narrow mechanical-valve exception, and abruptly stopping an effective AED can be more dangerous than its teratogenic risk). A third tier is comorbidity-specific: methylergonovine is fine in a normotensive patient but dangerous in preeclampsia/hypertension, and carboprost is fine unless the patient has asthma. Identify which tier a vignette is testing before picking an answer.

Surgical Disorders During Pregnancy 4%

Obstetrics and Gynecology Board Review

Every vignette in this topic is really testing one governing rule: pregnancy changes HOW you image and deliver, but it never changes WHETHER you treat a surgical emergency. Appendicitis, gallbladder disease, kidney stones, and adnexal torsion each get their own imaging ladder and their own uncomplicated-vs-complicated fork, but the fork always resolves the same way — uncomplicated disease can wait for a work-up, complicated disease (perforation, obstruction, infection, necrosis) is LIFE-SAVING and gets treated now, regardless of trimester. Delivery is a separate decision entirely, driven only by independent obstetric or fetal indications.

🧭Core Frameworks

Rule Zero: Pregnancy Never Delays Necessary Surgery
Indicated surgery + correct trimester-adjusted approach  >>  "let's wait until after delivery"
stabilize the mother first; choose the option with least fetal harm, not the option of no treatment
The single highest-yield principle in this topic: when a surgical disease is confirmed or highly suspected, you treat it — the TOS explicitly frames this as choosing the approach that minimizes fetal harm, not as delaying care because the patient is pregnant. Any answer choice that postpones a clearly indicated operation until postpartum "to protect the fetus" is almost always the trap, because untreated maternal sepsis or hemorrhage is far more dangerous to the fetus than a well-timed operation.
The Imaging Ladder: Ultrasound First, Then the Safer Second-Line Study
Ultrasound (every organ system)  →  nondiagnostic  →  MRI without gadolinium (or MRCP / MR urography)  →  CT is never withheld if maternal injury is major
US is always step one; contrast MRI (gadolinium) is avoided, but CT is not refused when it will change management of a major maternal injury
Appendicitis, biliary disease, renal stones, and adnexal torsion all start with ultrasound (plus Doppler for torsion). If ultrasound is nondiagnostic, the next step is MRI without gadolinium rather than a gadolinium-enhanced study. Trauma is the exception that trips people up: boards want you to know that CT should not be withheld purely to avoid fetal radiation when it is needed to diagnose a major maternal injury — an unstable or seriously injured mother is imaged appropriately.
Uncomplicated vs Complicated: The Fork That Decides Urgency
Uncomplicated (colic, unperforated appendicitis, stable stone, partial torsion) → observe / treat electively  |  Complicated (perforation, obstruction, infection, necrosis) → LIFE-SAVING, urgent surgery + IV antibiotics NOW
the same organ, two very different urgency levels
Every one of the four classic surgical disorders has this same two-tier structure: biliary colic is supportive, but cholangitis/choledocholithiasis is an urgent biliary emergency; an uncomplicated stone gets analgesia and hydration, but an infected obstructed stone is LIFE-SAVING and needs drainage plus IV antibiotics; uncomplicated appendicitis gets an appendectomy on a normal timeline, but perforated appendicitis with peritonitis needs urgent source control now. Recognizing which tier a vignette describes (fever + obstruction + peritonitis = complicated) is the core clinical-reasoning skill being tested.
Delivery Timing Follows Obstetric Indications, Not the Surgical Diagnosis
Surgical disease treated → pregnancy continues  |  Deliver ONLY for an independent fetal/maternal indication (severe abruption, uncontrolled hemorrhage, persistent Category III FHR, or a separately met obstetric threshold)
treating the appendix, gallbladder, stone, or torsed ovary is not itself a delivery order
None of appendicitis, cholelithiasis/cholecystitis, urolithiasis, or ovarian torsion automatically triggers delivery — the source is explicit that there is 'no automatic delivery' for any of these. Delivery happens only when a separate, independent obstetric or fetal indication is met (e.g., trauma causing severe abruption or a nonreassuring FHR pattern that persists despite resuscitation, or an unrelated threshold like severe preeclampsia at 34 weeks). Confusing 'we operated on the mother' with 'therefore we must deliver the baby' is a classic wrong-answer pattern.

Perinatal Infections 3%

Obstetrics and Gynecology Board Review

Perinatal infections boil down to three questions per organism: does it need routine screening, does it need intrapartum antibiotics, and does it change the delivery route. Once you sort GBS, syphilis, HSV, HIV, and HBV into those three buckets, the numeric cutoffs — 36 0/7–37 6/7 weeks, ROM ≥18 hours, HIV RNA >1,000 copies/mL — become the only things left to memorize.

🧭Core Frameworks

GBS: Who Gets Intrapartum Prophylaxis
Unknown status + (labor <37 wk OR ROM ≥18 h OR fever ≥38°C)  →  prophylaxis  |  GBS bacteriuria this pregnancy OR prior infant with invasive GBS disease  →  prophylaxis regardless of culture
a positive culture isn't the only trigger, and a scheduled CS with intact membranes cancels the need
Universal rectovaginal culture at 36 0/7–37 6/7 weeks is the default screen, but boards love the exception cases: if status is unknown when labor starts, three red-flag findings — very preterm labor, prolonged rupture of membranes, or intrapartum fever — each independently trigger empiric prophylaxis. Two groups get prophylaxis automatically regardless of any late culture: GBS bacteriuria during the current pregnancy, or a previous infant with invasive GBS disease. And the flip side is just as testable: a scheduled prelabor cesarean with intact membranes needs no GBS-specific prophylaxis at all, since there is no labor or ruptured membranes for transmission to occur through.
Syphilis: Stage Sets the Dose, Penicillin Is Never Optional
Primary / secondary / early latent  →  2.4 million U IM ×1  |  Late latent / unknown duration  →  2.4 million U IM weekly ×3  |  Neurosyphilis  →  IV penicillin q4h ×10–14 d
penicillin-allergic and pregnant → desensitize, don't substitute
Staging drives dosing: disease of known short duration gets a single dose, unknown-duration or late disease gets three weekly doses to cover a longer at-risk window, and CNS involvement needs high-dose IV therapy for 10–14 days. The classic trap is the allergy question — in pregnancy, penicillin is the only acceptable treatment for syphilis, and doxycycline is NOT an acceptable substitute, so an allergic patient is desensitized and then treated with penicillin rather than switched to another drug. Also expect a Jarisch–Herxheimer reaction 8–24 hours after treatment (fever, myalgias, possibly contractions or fetal heart rate changes) — this is an expected inflammatory response to dying spirochetes, not a reason to have delayed or to stop treatment.
"Infection ≠ Automatic Cesarean" — Know the Two Real Exceptions
GBS, syphilis, gonorrhea, chlamydia, HBV, condyloma, Triple I  →  NO infection-specific cesarean  |  HSV with active lesions/prodrome  →  cesarean  |  HIV RNA >1,000 copies/mL or unknown  →  scheduled cesarean at 38 wk
most perinatal infections are treated and delivered by usual obstetric indications, not sectioned
This is the single most testable pattern in the whole topic: almost every perinatal infection is managed with treatment and vaginal delivery when otherwise appropriate, not with surgery. Only two situations actually change the delivery route — active HSV lesions or prodrome at the onset of labor, and an unsuppressed or unknown HIV viral load above the 1,000 copies/mL threshold. Even Triple I (intra-amniotic infection) is managed with antibiotics and delivery once indicated on other grounds — the infection itself is not a cesarean indication, and neither is GBS colonization, syphilis, gonorrhea, chlamydia, HBV carriage, or condyloma acuminatum (unless warts cause outlet obstruction or a major bleeding risk).
HIV vs. HBV: Two Different Vertical-Transmission Strategies
HIV  →  suppress the mother (ART) + route decided by viral load  |  HBV  →  treat the mother if needed, but ALWAYS immunize the newborn (HBIG + vaccine ≤12 h)
one strategy is maternal-viral-load-dependent, the other is universal and neonate-directed
For HIV, prevention is two-pronged and load-dependent: effective combination ART throughout pregnancy for every patient, plus a delivery-route decision that hinges on a specific number — RNA >1,000 copies/mL, or an unknown load near delivery, means a scheduled cesarean at 38 weeks, while a well-suppressed load means vaginal delivery is generally fine. For HBV, the delivery route barely matters — no routine cesarean is needed to prevent transmission — because the intervention that actually works is universal, time-critical neonatal immunoprophylaxis: every infant of an HBsAg-positive mother gets HBIG plus hepatitis B vaccine within 12 hours of birth, regardless of how the infant was delivered or the mother's viral load.

Postpartum Care and Complications 4%

Obstetrics and Gynecology Board Review

Almost every postpartum question is a timing-plus-exam-finding puzzle: WHEN after delivery did this happen, and what does the uterus/breast/vital signs tell you? Bleeding sorts by whether the uterus is boggy or firm; fever sorts by whether it is hours (physiologic engorgement) or days (endometritis) after delivery; mood sorts by whether it has lasted more or less than two weeks. Learn the timing windows and the drug ladder's contraindications, and this topic becomes mostly recall.

🧭Core Frameworks

The Four T's of PPH — and the Boggy-vs-Firm Discriminator
Tone  →  Trauma  →  Tissue  →  Thrombin
boggy, enlarged uterus = Tone (atony); firm uterus + ongoing bleeding = Trauma until proven otherwise
Uterine atony (Tone) is the leading cause of postpartum hemorrhage, so it is always the first thing you check for and treat. But the fastest bedside sort is the uterine exam itself: a soft, boggy, enlarged uterus points to atony, while a firm, well-contracted uterus with continued brisk bleeding means the bleeding is coming from somewhere else — a cervical/vaginal laceration, hematoma, rupture, or inversion (Trauma). Only after Tone and Trauma are addressed do you work through incomplete placenta/retained products (Tissue) and bleeding out of proportion to exam findings, which suggests DIC/coagulopathy (Thrombin).
PPH Drug/Intervention Escalation Ladder
Massage + resuscitate (simultaneous)  →  Oxytocin (FIRST-LINE)  →  Methergine / Carboprost / Misoprostol (ALTERNATIVES)  →  Bakri/UAE  →  B-Lynch/O'Leary  →  Hysterectomy
the two contraindication traps: methergine in HTN/preeclampsia, carboprost in asthma
Uterine massage and hemorrhage-protocol resuscitation happen at the same time as — not before — drug therapy. Oxytocin (10 U IM, or 20 U/L IV crystalloid) is first-line with few postpartum contraindications. If atony persists, the alternative uterotonics each carry a named contraindication boards love to test: methylergonovine (0.2 mg IM) is contraindicated in hypertension/preeclampsia because it causes vasoconstriction, and carboprost (250 µg IM) is contraindicated/avoided in asthma because it can trigger bronchospasm; misoprostol (800 µg PR) has no such restriction and is useful when injectables are unavailable. Persistent atony escalates to tamponade (Bakri balloon) or uterine artery embolization in a stable, fertility-desiring patient, then uterine-sparing surgery (B-Lynch/O'Leary), and only for truly refractory hemorrhage, hysterectomy — the life-saving definitive step.
Postpartum Fever/Breast Timing Pattern
24–72 h engorgement (physiologic)  →  5–10 d endometritis (T ≥38.0°C + uterine tenderness ± foul lochia)  →  mastitis not improved by 48–72 h of antibiotics = abscess
the clock, not the fever alone, tells you whether this is normal or pathologic
Bilateral, diffuse breast firmness at 24–72 hours postpartum is physiologic engorgement from lactogenesis, not infection. A true postpartum infection has its own clock: endometritis classically appears 5–10 days postpartum (higher risk after cesarean) and is diagnosed by fever plus uterine tenderness, treated with IV clindamycin plus gentamicin until afebrile for 24–48 hours. Mastitis is treated with dicloxacillin while breastfeeding/pumping is continued (not stopped); if it fails to improve after 48–72 hours of antibiotics, imaging is obtained to evaluate for an abscess needing drainage.
Postpartum Contraception Timing (WHO MEC Logic)
Progestin-only pill / implant / IUD  →  safe immediately postpartum  |  Combined hormonal  →  ≥3 wk (no VTE risk) / ≥6 wk (VTE risk)  |  Puerperal sepsis  →  MEC 4 for IUD insertion
estrogen = added clotting risk during the highest postpartum VTE-risk window
Progestin-only methods, the implant, and IUDs (copper or LNG) can generally be started immediately postpartum and are compatible with breastfeeding. Combined hormonal methods are the exception: the early postpartum period itself is a high-VTE-risk state, and adding estrogen on top of that (plus a possible reduction in milk supply) is why they are deferred — at least 3 weeks if the patient has no additional VTE risk factors, at least 6 weeks if she does. Puerperal sepsis is its own hard stop for IUD insertion (WHO MEC 4 — unacceptable risk), independent of the general postpartum timing rules.

Benign Disorders of the Lower Genital Tract 3%

Obstetrics and Gynecology Board Review

Almost everything here is asymptomatic-until-triggered: cysts you observe until they aren't, lesions you biopsy only past a defined threshold, and a route of delivery that stays vaginal unless a lesion is mechanically obstructive. Lock in the site map, the lichen triad, the Bartholin escalation ladder, and the universal biopsy triggers, and this whole low-weight topic turns into fast, pattern-matched points.

🧭Core Frameworks

The Site Map: Location Is the Diagnosis
Nabothian → CERVIX  |  Gartner → lateral VAGINAL WALL  |  Skene → PERIURETHRAL  |  Bartholin → posterolateral INTROITUS
name the site, name the cyst
Four benign cystic lesions of the lower genital tract are really an anatomy question wearing a pathology costume. A mucus-filled bump on the cervix (roughly 3 mm-3 cm) is a Nabothian cyst, a blocked endocervical gland that needs no treatment unless it is large, symptomatic, or blocks your view of the cervix. A cyst along the lateral wall of the vagina is a Gartner duct cyst, a mesonephric (Wolffian) duct remnant that is observed unless symptomatic. A cyst next to the urethral meatus is a Skene gland cyst, observed if asymptomatic and marsupialized or excised if it persists. A cyst or abscess at the posterolateral introitus is Bartholin gland disease. Once you place the lesion on the map, the rest of the question is usually just observe vs. drain vs. excise.
The Lichen Triad: White, Thick, or Erosive
SCLEROSUS — thin white atrophic plaques, architecture loss, 4%-6% SCC risk  →  clobetasol 0.05%  |  SIMPLEX CHRONICUS — thick leathery itch-scratch skin  →  remove irritant + high-potency steroid  |  PLANUS — erosive/papulosquamous, can extend into the VAGINA  →  ultrapotent steroid ± vaginal steroid/dilators
white+scarred = LS, thick+itchy = LSC, erosive+vaginal = LP
All three vulvar dermatoses respond to topical corticosteroids, so boards test which LOOKS like which and which one CAN involve the vagina. Lichen sclerosus is the thin, white, architecture-destroying disease with a real (4%-6% per this source) lifetime SCC risk, so it needs a structured clobetasol taper — 4 weeks nightly → 4 weeks every other night → 4 weeks twice weekly — plus lifelong surveillance every 6-12 months. Lichen simplex chronicus looks the opposite — thick, leathery, hyperkeratotic skin from a chronic itch-scratch cycle — and responds to removing the irritant plus a high-potency steroid; if it has not improved in about 1-3 weeks, biopsy instead of persisting with empiric treatment. Lichen planus is erosive/papulosquamous/hypertrophic and, unlike classic lichen sclerosus, can involve the vagina itself, occasionally causing vaginal stenosis that needs dilators or specialist systemic therapy.
Bartholin Disease: Escalate Only As Far As Needed
Asymptomatic cyst → observe/sitz baths  →  Symptomatic cyst/abscess → Word catheter (balloon 2-3 mL saline, left 4-6 weeks)  →  Recurrence → marsupialization  →  New mass, age >40 → BIOPSY at presentation
the algorithm resets the moment malignancy is on the table
Bartholin gland disease is a single progression — asymptomatic cyst, symptomatic cyst, abscess, recurrent lesion, suspicious lesion — and management climbs one rung at a time. A small (roughly 1-2 cm), asymptomatic cyst is simply observed. A symptomatic cyst or abscess gets a Word catheter: a small balloon catheter inflated with 2-3 mL of sterile saline and left in place 4-6 weeks so a permanent epithelialized drainage tract can form. If the lesion keeps recurring, marsupialization is next, and a repeatedly recurrent or solid lesion may need outright excision and biopsy. Age overrides the ladder: any NEW Bartholin-area mass in a patient older than 40 skips straight to biopsy at presentation, because a solid mass at that age must exclude Bartholin gland or vulvar carcinoma before it is treated as routine cystic disease.
The Universal Biopsy Triggers
New / thickened / nodular / ulcerated / CHANGING lesion  OR  new Bartholin mass age >40  OR  LSC not improved in ~1-3 weeks  →  BIOPSY before more destructive or empiric therapy
reassurance and topical steroids have an expiration date
Nearly every lesion in this topic is managed conservatively — until a short list of red flags appears, at which point the correct answer flips from reassure/treat to biopsy first. Recognize three triggers instantly: (1) any vulvar or cervical lesion that is new, thickened, nodular, ulcerated, or actively changing; (2) a new Bartholin-area mass in a woman older than 40; and (3) lichen simplex chronicus that fails to improve after roughly 1-3 weeks of appropriate topical therapy. None of this topic's benign lesions — lichen sclerosus, LSC, lichen planus, Bartholin/Skene/Gartner/Nabothian cysts — routinely change delivery timing or route on their own, and none is itself a cesarean indication; the only exception is a lesion so anatomically large or stenotic (a giant obstructive Gartner cyst, severe lichen-planus vaginal stenosis) that it mechanically blocks the birth canal.

Almost everything here is really three small decision trees dressed up as different anatomy: does the uterus's EXTERNAL contour look normal or abnormal, does the fibroid touch the cavity or not, and does the endometrium's age/thickness/histology demand a biopsy right now. Nail the branch point and the number attached to it, and most vignettes in this topic collapse into a two-step lookup rather than a memorization problem.

🧭Core Frameworks

Müllerian Anomaly Pattern: Contour Predicts the Complication
Septate (contour NORMAL)  →  1st-trimester loss (~25%)  |  Bicornuate / Unicornuate / Didelphys (contour ABNORMAL)  →  preterm birth + malpresentation
check the EXTERNAL fundal contour to separate septate from bicornuate
A septate uterus is a failure of the midline septum to resorb, not a failure of the two Müllerian ducts to fuse — so its outside contour is normal and convex, but the fibrous, poorly vascularized septum inside is a bad place to implant, producing recurrent first-trimester loss (~25% in the source). Bicornuate, unicornuate, and didelphys uteri are true fusion/development abnormalities, so the outside contour is genuinely abnormal (clefted or duplicated) and the cavity itself is smaller or misshapen — this predisposes to preterm labor and malpresentation rather than early loss. That is why definitive treatment differs: septate uterus with recurrent loss gets hysteroscopic septum excision (only after imaging excludes a bicornuate contour), while bicornuate/unicornuate anatomy is usually just monitored expectantly through pregnancy.
FIGO Fibroid Map: Location Predicts Symptom and Surgical Route
FIGO 0–2 (submucosal, touches cavity)  →  bleeding + implantation failure  →  hysteroscopic myomectomy  |  FIGO 3–7 (intramural/subserosal)  →  bulk symptoms  →  myomectomy (fertility desired) or hysterectomy (fertility complete)
does it distort the cavity? that single question predicts the symptom AND the operation
FIGO types 0, 1, and 2 all encroach on or sit inside the endometrial cavity, so they present with abnormal bleeding and an implantation/infertility problem, and they are removed hysteroscopically. Types 3 through 7 progressively spare the cavity and mostly cause bulk/pressure symptoms instead. From there, management is driven by fertility desire, not just the fibroid: a small asymptomatic intramural fibroid with fertility desired is watched expectantly, a symptomatic fibroid with fertility desired gets a myomectomy, and a fibroid with no future fertility desired is definitively treated with hysterectomy (uterine artery embolization is an option here but is avoided if future pregnancy is planned, since it can compromise uterine/ovarian perfusion). Actively growing fibroids under observation are reimaged about every 6 months.
Endometrial Hyperplasia Risk Ladder
Simple (1%) → Complex (3%) → Simple atypical (8%) → Complex atypical (29%) → EIN (27–52%)
atypia — not architectural complexity — is what makes the risk jump
Walking this ladder, the risk barely moves from simple to complex hyperplasia (1% to 3%, both non-atypical), but jumps sharply the moment atypia appears (3% to 8%, then all the way to 29% for complex atypical hyperplasia). EIN is treated as its own premalignant category with an alarmingly high 27–52% risk of coexisting or progressing to cancer in the source. That risk ladder is exactly what dictates management: benign, non-atypical hyperplasia is treated with a progestin trial for 3–6 months followed by a repeat endometrial biopsy, while EIN calls for definitive total hysterectomy in the source's key points, with fertility-sparing progestin reserved for patients who accept strict serial sampling.
Two Separate Triggers That Both Mean 'Sample the Endometrium Now'
AUB, age ≥45 → endometrial biopsy at presentation  |  Postmenopausal bleeding + endometrial stripe ≥4 mm → tissue sampling
one trigger is an age cutoff, the other is a thickness cutoff — they are not interchangeable
Age ≥45 with abnormal uterine bleeding earns an endometrial biopsy at presentation regardless of what an ultrasound shows — age alone is the trigger. After menopause, the trigger instead becomes an ultrasound finding: a bleeding postmenopausal patient with an endometrial stripe ≥4 mm goes on to tissue sampling in the source's algorithm. Endometrial polyps sit in this same family of decisions — they carry a low but real malignant potential (about 1–2% premenopausal, roughly 5% postmenopausal) — so a postmenopausal polyp is always removed and sent to pathology, even when it looks entirely benign on imaging.

Contraception and Family Planning 18%

Obstetrics and Gynecology Board Review

Contraception is the single highest-weighted topic in this Obstetrics and Gynecology review, but it rewards pattern recognition over rote memorization: methods that need no daily user action (IUDs, implants, sterilization) are consistently the most effective, MEC category 4 marks a short, memorizable list of estrogen deal-breakers, and emergency contraception is just a timing ladder. Anchor these four lenses and nearly every vignette — across natural methods, barriers, IUDs, combined and progestin-only hormones, emergency contraception, and permanent methods — becomes sortable rather than something to memorize method-by-method.

🧭Core Frameworks

The Efficacy Ladder: Less User Action → Lower Failure
Natural/Barrier (~15%–45% typical failure) > Short-acting hormonal — pill/patch/ring/POP (~8%) > DMPA (~3%) > LARC — implant/IUD (<1%) > Permanent (<0.3%)
the less a method depends on the user remembering something, the lower its real-world failure rate
Perfect-use efficacy is similar across many methods on paper, but ACTUAL-use failure diverges sharply because it tracks how much daily/weekly effort a method demands. Natural family planning and barrier methods need action at every act of intercourse; short-acting hormonal methods need daily-to-monthly adherence; DMPA needs a visit every 3 months; LARC and permanent methods need essentially nothing after placement. This is exactly why the source flags LARC as first-line postpartum strategy: it removes user error from the equation entirely.
WHO / US MEC: The 1–4 Risk Scale
1 = no restriction → 2 = benefits generally outweigh risks, usually use → 3 = risks usually outweigh benefits, usually avoid → 4 = unacceptable health risk, DO NOT USE
memorize the short list of MEC-4 triggers for combined hormonal contraception (CHC)
MEC 4 for CHC clusters around vascular/thrombotic danger: migraine WITH aura, blood pressure ≥160/100 mmHg, known vascular disease (VTE/PE, CAD, stroke), hepatocellular adenoma or malignant hepatoma, and smoking ≥15 cigarettes/day past age 35. Progestin-only methods and IUDs are almost never MEC 4 for these same conditions — which is exactly why they are the fallback whenever CHC is disqualified.
The Method-Selection Algorithm
Exclude pregnancy → STI protection needed? use condom → estrogen contraindicated? route to Cu-IUD/LNG-IUD/implant/DMPA/POP per MEC → wants lowest user-error? IUD/implant → family complete? permanent contraception only after LARC counseling
this branching sequence, not a features list, is what most vignettes are actually testing
Every high-yield contraception vignette is a walk down this decision tree. A stem that mentions multiple partners is testing branch 2 (condoms). A stem with migraine-with-aura or severe hypertension is testing branch 3 (estrogen exclusion). A stem emphasizing 'wants to be done having children but isn't 100% sure' is testing the last branch, where the correct answer is LARC, not sterilization, because sterilization should be reserved for patients certain about permanence.
Emergency Contraception: A Timing Ladder, Not a Menu
≤72h: levonorgestrel 1.5 mg PO once (first-line) → 72–120h: ulipristal 30 mg PO once (more effective than LNG in this window) → ≤120h + wants ongoing method: copper IUD (99.8% efficacy, most effective EC overall)
earlier oral dosing is always better; the copper IUD's efficacy barely decays across the full 5-day window
EC choice is driven almost entirely by elapsed time since intercourse, not by patient preference alone. Levonorgestrel's efficacy falls off the later it is taken, which is why ulipristal becomes source-preferred in the 72–120 hour window. The copper IUD sits above both oral options at any point up to 120 hours and additionally converts a one-time rescue into ongoing 10-year contraception.

Infections of the Female Reproductive Tract, Including STDs 3%

Obstetrics and Gynecology Board Review

Every infection here reduces to the same move: match a numeric cutoff (pH, fever, Amsel score, dose) to a single hallmark finding, then pick the one first-line drug. Syphilis is one drug — penicillin — on three different schedules; PID is one diagnostic threshold that triggers immediate empiric treatment; and pregnancy rarely changes the diagnosis, it just forces a drug swap and, for HSV alone, a delivery-route rule.

🧭Core Frameworks

The Vaginitis Triad: pH + One Hallmark Sign
BV: pH >4.5 + clue cells >20% + Amsel 3/4  |  Candida: pH ≤4.5 + pseudohyphae  |  Trich: pH 6–7 + motile flagellates
same wet mount, three different pH ranges
All three cause discharge, but a single pH check plus one classic finding separates them instantly. BV raises pH above 4.5 with clue cells and a positive whiff test (any 3 of 4 Amsel criteria, or a Nugent score of 7–10). Candida keeps pH normal-to-low (≤4.5) with pruritus and pseudohyphae/budding yeast. Trichomonas pushes pH even higher (6–7) with motile flagellates and a strawberry cervix. Treatment mirrors the organism — metronidazole for BV, a topical azole for Candida, metronidazole 2 g once for Trich — and Trich is the only one of the three that requires routine partner treatment.
PID: One Diagnostic Threshold, Then Treat Immediately
Sexually active + pelvic/lower abdominal pain + ≥1 of {cervical-motion, uterine, or adnexal tenderness} → start empiric treatment now
don't wait for NAAT results before treating
PID has a deliberately low diagnostic bar: pelvic pain plus just one tenderness finding on exam is enough to start treatment. Supportive findings — fever >38.3°C, mucopurulent discharge, abundant WBCs on saline microscopy, elevated ESR/CRP, or positive GC/CT — raise confidence but are not required, because under-treating PID is the costlier mistake given its reproductive sequelae. Outpatient regimens center on ceftriaxone plus doxycycline (add metronidazole if BV/Trich coexists); inpatient regimens — for pregnancy, TOA, severe illness, or outpatient failure — use cefoxitin or cefotetan plus doxycycline, with clindamycin plus gentamicin as the substitute for cephalosporin allergy or for pregnant patients.
Syphilis: One Drug, Three Schedules
Primary/secondary/early latent → 2.4 million U IM ×1  |  Late latent/unknown duration → 2.4 million U IM weekly ×3  |  Neurosyphilis → 3–4 million U IV q4h ×10–14 d
the drug never changes — only the schedule and route do
Every stage of syphilis is treated with penicillin, so exam questions test the schedule, not the drug. Early disease (a painless chancre, a generalized rash often on palms/soles, or early latent infection acquired under a year ago) needs a single intramuscular dose. Once duration is unknown or exceeds a year (late latent), the regimen escalates to weekly dosing for three doses; CNS or ocular involvement escalates further to a prolonged IV course. In pregnancy, penicillin stays mandatory — a penicillin-allergic pregnant patient with syphilis is desensitized and then still given penicillin, because no alternative reliably prevents congenital syphilis.
Pregnancy Changes the Drug and the Delivery Route, Not the Diagnosis
Active HSV lesion/prodrome at labor → cesarean  |  GC/CT/BV/Trich/syphilis alone → no cesarean indication  |  Avoid tetracyclines & fluoroquinolones in pregnancy
HSV is the only one of these infections that changes the delivery plan by itself
Most reproductive-tract infections are diagnosed the same way whether or not a patient is pregnant — pregnancy mainly forces a drug swap (avoid doxycycline/tetracyclines and fluoroquinolones; use pregnancy-compatible regimens) rather than a different diagnosis. The one true delivery-route rule is HSV: an active genital lesion or prodromal symptoms at the onset of labor mandates cesarean delivery, while a history of HSV without current lesions or prodrome permits vaginal delivery. Gonorrhea, chlamydia, BV, trichomoniasis, and syphilis alone are never automatic cesarean indications, and PID or TOA in pregnancy is managed with hospitalization and antibiotics — delivery timing stays an obstetric decision, not an infection-driven one.

Obstetrics and Gynecology Board Review

Learn the hymen as your ruler, the three DeLancey levels as your anatomy map, and the six leak patterns as your history-taking checklist, and the rest of this topic is just plugging numbers and drug names into slots you already know.

🧭Core Frameworks

POP-Q Staging — the Hymen Is the Ruler
Stage 0: no prolapse  →  I: >1 cm ABOVE hymen  →  II: within 1 cm of hymen  →  III: >1 cm BELOW hymen  →  IV: complete eversion
every stage is defined as a distance from one fixed landmark
POP-Q measures the most-distal point of prolapse relative to the hymen: negative numbers are above it, positive numbers are below it. Stage II is the trap zone most students miss — it is not 'at the hymen,' it is anywhere within 1 cm on either side of it. The older Baden-Walker system grades descent the same direction (halfway to hymen → reaches hymen → halfway beyond → complete eversion) but boards mainly want you fluent in POP-Q's hymen-referenced logic.
DeLancey's Three Levels of Support
Level I (cardinal + uterosacral ligaments) → apex  |  Level II (endopelvic fascia) → mid-vagina  |  Level III (perineal body) → distal vagina
where support fails tells you which structure prolapsed
Each level has its own failure pattern: Level I loss gives apical/uterine/vault prolapse, Level II loss gives a cystocele (anterior) or rectocele (posterior), and Level III loss gives a urethrocele or perineocele. The classic trap: hysterectomy removes the uterus but does nothing to restore Level I ligamentous support, so hysterectomy alone does not fix (and can even unmask) apical/vault prolapse — that needs a dedicated apical suspension.
Pregnancy Rule: Support It, Don't Fix It
Symptomatic POP or SUI in pregnancy → pessary / conservative management → reassess AFTER postpartum recovery; elective sling, Burch, hysteropexy, and colporrhaphy all WAIT
pregnancy is a temporary, self-resolving anatomic state — don't operate on a moving target
Pregnancy-associated SUI frequently improves on its own after delivery, and reconstructive tissues are still remodeling, so the source treats reconstructive surgery (sling, Burch, uterosacral suspension, hysteropexy) as something to defer until postpartum, not something to fix during pregnancy. Neither prolapse alone nor incontinence alone changes delivery timing — the only exception is severe nonreducible prolapse causing true obstruction, which needs individualized planning.
The SUI Ladder Has No Drug Rung
Behavioral (weight loss, Kegels, pelvic-floor PT) → pessary → midurethral sling (definitive) → Burch / bladder-neck sling (alternative) → bulking agent (only for intrinsic sphincter deficiency)
there is no FDA-approved medication for stress incontinence in this source
Stress incontinence escalates straight from behavioral therapy to a mechanical fix — pessary, then a sling as the standard definitive operation — with no pharmacologic step in between. That drug rung exists only on the urgency-incontinence ladder (antimuscarinics, mirabegron). If an answer choice offers a medication for pure stress incontinence, it is designed to be wrong.

Reproductive Endocrinology, Infertility, and Menopause 4%

Obstetrics and Gynecology Board Review

This topic feels like a wall of hormone names, but almost every question collapses to one move: is the gonadotropin (FSH) high, low, or normal, and is estrogen high or low? That single pattern sorts an anovulatory patient into one of the WHO groups, tells you whether menopause has arrived, and tells you which drug is safe to add. Rotterdam PCOS, POI cutoffs, and the MHT prescribing rule all run on that same estrogen-status logic — nail the pattern-matching and the specific numbers become easy recall on top of it.

🧭Core Frameworks

WHO Classification of Anovulatory Infertility
Group I: hypogonadotropic, hypoestrogenic (hypothalamic amenorrhea)  →  Group II: normogonadotropic, normoestrogenic (PCOS)  →  Group III: hypergonadotropic, hypoestrogenic (POI)  →  Group IV: hyperprolactinemic (prolactinoma)
the FSH + estrogen pattern IS the diagnosis, before you ever image the ovaries
Group I patients have low FSH and low estrogen because the hypothalamus/pituitary is under-driving the ovary — classically from caloric deficit, excessive exercise, or stress; the fix is correcting the drive, not giving fertility drugs first. Group II (PCOS) has normal-range FSH and normal-range estrogen but disordered ovulation, usually with hyperandrogenism. Group III (POI) is the mirror image of Group I: the pituitary is screaming (high FSH) because the ovary isn't listening (low estrogen). Group IV is a separate axis entirely — prolactin excess suppresses GnRH pulsatility and causes anovulation regardless of the FSH/estrogen numbers. A board vignette that gives you an FSH and an estradiol is handing you the answer directly.
PCOS — Rotterdam 2-of-3
Oligo/anovulation  +  clinical or biochemical hyperandrogenism  +  polycystic ovarian morphology  →  diagnosis needs ANY 2 of these 3, after excluding mimics
polycystic ovaries are NOT required if the other two criteria are present
The Rotterdam criteria are an exclusion diagnosis dressed up as a checklist: you still have to rule out thyroid disease, hyperprolactinemia, and nonclassic congenital adrenal hyperplasia before you're allowed to call it PCOS. The most commonly tested trap is assuming an ultrasound is mandatory — a patient with oligo-ovulation plus clinical hyperandrogenism (hirsutism, acne) already meets 2 of 3 and needs no ovarian imaging to diagnose. Once diagnosed, if the patient does not currently want pregnancy, the endometrium still needs protection from unopposed estrogen exposure (cyclic or continuous progestin, or a hormonal IUD) because chronic anovulation means chronic unopposed estrogen.
The Infertility Clock & Who Gets Tested
Female partner <35 yr  →  evaluate after 12 mo of regular unprotected intercourse  |  ≥35 yr  →  evaluate after 6 mo  |  known risk factor (irregular cycles, endometriosis, PID, uterine anomaly)  →  evaluate sooner
always work up BOTH partners — semen analysis is the first-line male test, HSG checks tubal patency
The age cutoff exists because ovarian reserve declines meaningfully after 35, so boards shorten the waiting period to avoid wasting reproductive time on watchful waiting. A patient with a known risk factor (irregular cycles suggesting anovulation, a history of PID or endometriosis, a known uterine anomaly) should never be told to 'just wait' the full window — she goes straight to evaluation. The classic trap is working up only the female partner: a full infertility evaluation is symmetric, with semen analysis as the cheap, first-line male test done in parallel with (not after) the female workup, and HSG used specifically to assess tubal patency, not ovulation.
Menopause Definition & the MHT Prescribing Rule
Menopause = 12 mo of amenorrhea after the final period (avg age 51, FSH >40 IU/L)  →  moderate-severe vasomotor symptoms + no contraindication = MHT is first-line  →  intact uterus: estrogen + progestin  |  no uterus: estrogen alone
unopposed estrogen with a uterus in place is always the wrong answer
Menopause is a retrospective, clinical diagnosis — you need 12 full months of amenorrhea, not a single lab value, though an FSH persistently above 40 IU/L supports the diagnosis. Once confirmed, the single highest-yield prescribing rule is whether the uterus is present: any patient with a uterus who receives systemic estrogen must also receive a progestin to prevent endometrial hyperplasia/cancer, while a patient without a uterus (post-hysterectomy) can safely take estrogen alone. MHT is contraindicated in pregnancy, estrogen-dependent neoplasm, active/prior venous thromboembolism, chronic liver impairment, and unexplained uterine bleeding until that bleeding is evaluated — and it is never indicated purely for cardiovascular or dementia prevention.

Gynecologic Oncology 4%

Obstetrics and Gynecology Board Review

Every gyn cancer question collapses to two moves: name the trigger symptom (postcoital bleeding, postmenopausal bleeding, a complex adnexal mass, a nonhealing vulvar sore) and then place the disease on a surgery-versus-radiation-versus-chemo axis using one numeric cutoff. Learn where each cancer's line is drawn — parametrial invasion, a 4-mm endometrial stripe, 1 cm of residual tumor, 1 mm of stromal invasion — and the staging tables stop being memorization and start being logic.

🧭Core Frameworks

Cervical Cancer: Where Surgery Stops and Chemoradiation Starts
IA1, no LVSI: cone (fertility desired) or simple hysterectomy (fertility not desired)  →  IA1+LVSI through IIA2: radical hysterectomy + pelvic nodes OR radiation  →  IIB and beyond: cisplatin-based chemoradiation + intracavitary radiation
parametrial invasion (Stage IIB) is the hard line — cross it and surgery is off the table
Cervical cancer management is a ladder, not a list. Microinvasive disease (IA1) is treated almost like a high-grade dysplasia — a cone or simple hysterectomy suffices because the risk of spread is minimal. Once invasion is deeper or lymphovascular space invasion (LVSI) is present, through Stage IIA2, radical surgery (removing parametria and nodes) or primary radiation are both reasonable. But the moment tumor invades the parametrium — Stage IIB by definition — surgery is no longer curative on its own, and cisplatin-based chemoradiation with intracavitary brachytherapy becomes the answer for IIB through IVA. This single cutoff, parametrial invasion, is the fact boards hang the whole staging table on.
Endometrial Cancer: Bleeding Triggers Biopsy, Biopsy Triggers a Fork
Postmenopausal bleeding + stripe >4 mm (or persistent bleeding despite a thin stripe) → endometrial biopsy → fertility completed: TH-BSO + surgical staging  |  fertility desired + very early low-grade disease: progestin therapy + strict serial EMB
fertility-sparing treatment suppresses disease — it does not cure it, so sampling never stops
Any postmenopausal bleeding demands evaluation, and an endometrial stripe over 4 mm on that workup is the trigger for tissue diagnosis. AUB in a woman 45 or older, or a younger woman with chronic unopposed estrogen exposure, gets the same sampling logic. Once atypical hyperplasia (EIN) or early low-grade cancer is diagnosed, the fork is fertility: if childbearing is complete, definitive total hysterectomy with staging is first-line. If fertility is desired and disease is selected/early enough, progestin therapy with strict serial biopsies is the alternative — but this is medical suppression of hormonally responsive tissue, not a cure, so surveillance sampling continues indefinitely. Lynch syndrome carriers get their own number: annual endometrial biopsy starting around age 35.
Ovarian Cancer: Stage It Surgically, Debulk to ≤1 cm, Then Chemo
TAH-BSO + omentectomy + peritoneal washings + full surgical staging + maximal cytoreduction (optimal = residual disease ≤1 cm) → carboplatin + paclitaxel
CA-125 is for monitoring response and detecting recurrence — never a population screening test
Ovarian and fallopian-tube cancer is staged surgically, not radiographically, which is why the first-line operation does double duty: it removes disease and it tells you the stage. 'Optimal' cytoreduction is a specific number — residual tumor of 1 cm or less — and the amount of tumor left behind is one of the strongest prognostic factors in this disease. Systemic therapy afterward is a platinum-taxane doublet, carboplatin plus paclitaxel. CA-125 is genuinely useful, but only for tracking a known cancer's response to treatment or catching recurrence early — using it as a general population screening test is a classic wrong answer because it lacks the specificity to justify that role.
One Marker, One Tumor: The Germ-Cell / Sex-Cord Panel
LDH → dysgerminoma  |  AFP → yolk-sac tumor  |  AFP + hCG → embryonal carcinoma  |  hCG → nongestational choriocarcinoma  |  Inhibin (± estradiol) → granulosa-cell tumor  |  Androgens → Sertoli–Leydig tumor
chemo for germ-cell malignancy = BEP (bleomycin + etoposide + cisplatin); Stage IA dysgerminoma and selected immature teratomas are exceptions
These tumors mostly strike young women who want to keep their fertility, so the surgical default is unilateral salpingo-oophorectomy with complete staging rather than bilateral removal, when the disease is unilateral and fertility is desired. Each tumor secretes a near-signature marker, which is exactly how boards test recognition: a young woman with a pelvic mass and an isolated LDH elevation is a dysgerminoma until proven otherwise; an elevated AFP points to yolk-sac tumor; a granulosa-cell tumor produces estrogen (think endometrial hyperplasia and Call–Exner bodies on histology) and can recur 15–20 years later, so surveillance must be prolonged; a Sertoli–Leydig tumor produces androgens and presents with virilization. When chemotherapy is needed, BEP is the regimen, with Stage IA dysgerminoma and some immature teratomas able to skip it.

Obstetrics and Gynecology Board Review

Almost every menstrual-cycle vignette collapses into the same three moves: beta-hCG first to exclude pregnancy, PALM-COEIN to name the mechanism (structural lesion you can see vs. a nonstructural physiologic problem), and then an age or biopsy trigger that tells you whether this is a treat-and-watch problem or a tissue-diagnosis problem. 🧭Core Frameworks

The Universal First Step: Exclude Pregnancy, Then Classify
Any abnormal bleeding or amenorrhea → β-hCG FIRST → if pregnancy excluded, sort as PALM (structural, seen on imaging/pathology) or COEIN (nonstructural, functional)
a stem that skips straight to hormone therapy without mentioning pregnancy testing is testing whether you noticed the omission
Before any hormonal or surgical decision, every reproductive-age patient with abnormal uterine bleeding or amenorrhea gets a beta-hCG. This single step prevents two classic errors: giving high-dose estrogen to an unrecognized pregnancy, and working up 'AUB' that is actually a threatened abortion or ectopic. Naming the Bleeding Pattern by the Numbers
Normal cycle 21–35 d, flow 3–5 d, loss 30–50 mL  |  HMB >80 mL OR >7 d  |  Oligomenorrhea >35 d between cycles  |  Frequent cycles <21 d  |  2° amenorrhea 3 mo (was regular) or 6 mo (was irregular)  |  1° amenorrhea: no menarche by age 15
the source keeps two different amenorrhea thresholds across chapters -- know both, don't merge them
Every pattern name is defined by a number, and boards will describe the pattern in a stem and expect you to attach the correct label. A cycle repeating every 21-35 days with 3-5 days of flow and roughly 30-50 mL of loss is normal; call it heavy menstrual bleeding once loss exceeds 80 mL or duration exceeds 7 days. Cycles spacing out past 35 days are oligomenorrhea; cycles arriving sooner than every 21 days are frequent. AUB age >45 → endometrial biopsy  |  Younger patient + major endometrial-cancer risk factor → biopsy regardless of age  |  Postmenopausal bleeding + stripe around 4 mm or persistent/recurrent bleeding → sampling/hysteroscopy even if an initial study was reassuring
age plus risk factors, not age alone, decide who needs tissue -- and a normal ultrasound does not end the workup if bleeding persists
The single highest-yield decision point in this topic is knowing who gets an endometrial biopsy. Any woman with AUB over age 45 gets one. A younger woman gets one anyway if she carries a major endometrial-cancer risk factor (obesity, chronic anovulation/PCOS, unopposed estrogen exposure, tamoxifen, Lynch syndrome) -- age alone should never be the reason to skip biopsy in a high-risk patient. In postmenopausal women, an endometrial stripe around the 4 mm threshold prompts sampling, and critically, persistent or recurrent postmenopausal bleeding still warrants biopsy or hysteroscopy even after an initially reassuring ultrasound -- a normal first look does not close the case if the bleeding keeps happening.
Hyperplasia's One Branch Point: Atypia Present or Absent

atypia is the fork in the road; ablation is never an acceptable substitute for hysterectomy once atypia/EIN is diagnosed
Hyperplasia management hinges entirely on whether cytologic atypia is present. Without atypia, progression risk is low (simple hyperplasia about 1%, complex about 3%), so first-line treatment is progestin for 3-6 months followed by repeat endometrial biopsy. Once atypia is present -- whether called simple atypical (about 8%), complex atypical (about 29%), or EIN (27-52% coexistent or progression risk) -- the calculus changes: a patient whose fertility is complete goes to definitive hysterectomy, while one who still wants fertility gets high-dose progestin with strict, frequent serial sampling instead. What is never acceptable for atypia/EIN is endometrial ablation, supracervical hysterectomy, or power morcellation -- all three can leave malignant tissue behind or destroy the ability to fully evaluate it.

Contemporary Issues 3%

Obstetrics and Gynecology Board Review

Contemporary Issues is less a single disease and more a collection of 'what do you do right now' cutoffs and 'when do you deliver' rules pulled from across obstetrics and gynecology. The trick is not memorizing every number in isolation — it's recognizing that most of these facts cluster into a small number of decision patterns: severity ladders that reset the delivery clock, load-then-maintenance emergency drugs, and age- or category-based imaging/screening rules. 🧭Core Frameworks

The Hypertensive Ladder: HTN → Preeclampsia → Severe → Eclampsia
Gestational HTN (BP≥140/90 ×2, ≥4h apart, no proteinuria) → Preeclampsia (+proteinuria ≥300mg/24h or P/C≥0.3) → Preeclampsia WITH severe features (BP≥160/110, Plt<100k, Cr>1.1 or doubled, LFT≥2×ULN, pulmonary edema, cerebral/visual sx) → Eclampsia (+seizure)
each rung raises the urgency AND moves the delivery clock earlier
Boards test this as a ladder, not a list: the label only matters because it sets the delivery week. Gestational HTN and preeclampsia without severe features both wait to 37 weeks under surveillance. The instant any severe-feature criterion appears, the clock jumps to 34 weeks if the mother is stable, and magnesium sulfate starts. Eclampsia has no fixed week at all — you stabilize the seizure first, then deliver regardless of gestational age.
Three OB Emergencies, Three First-Line Drugs
Eclampsia → MgSO₂ 6 g IV over 15–20 min → 2 g/h infusion (recurrent seizure: 2 g IV over 3–5 min)  |  PPH → uterine massage + oxytocin 10 U IM or 20 U in 1 L IV  |  Preterm labor → nifedipine 10 mg PO q15min ×1 h → 10–30 mg q4–6h (max 180 mg/day)
memorize the load-then-maintenance pair for each drug, not just the drug name
Every one of these three obstetric emergencies has a single first-line agent with a load-then-maintenance structure. Two classic contraindication traps ride along: methylergonovine (an alternative PPH agent) is contraindicated in hypertension/preeclampsia, and carboprost (another PPH alternative) is contraindicated or avoided in asthma. The diagnostic step needs two or more abnormal values on the 100-g OGTT — one abnormal value alone does not make the diagnosis. Once diagnosed, insulin is first-line pharmacotherapy; metformin is the fallback when insulin isn't feasible, and glyburide is specifically flagged as not preferred. The A1-vs-A2 split (diet-controlled vs medication-requiring) then drives both fetal surveillance timing (roughly 32–36 weeks for controlled A2) and the delivery week.

Pregnancy or lactation is never a reason to delay working up a persistent suspicious mass.

The prepubertal vaginal tract is thin and unestrogenized, so nonspecific irritation is overwhelmingly the most common finding — that single fact explains why hygiene and observation are correct so often in this topic. A short list of red flags (foul-plus-bloody discharge, unexplained bleeding especially under age 6, a persistent or necrotic lesion, a new mass) is what flips the answer from reassurance to a specific next step. Learn the handful of conservative-first management ladders and the numbers that override them, and this whole low-weight topic becomes fast pattern recognition.

🧭Core Frameworks

Triage the Prepubertal Genital Complaint
Nonspecific discharge (thin, unestrogenized mucosa) → Vulvovaginitis, hygiene + sitz baths ~2 wk  |  Foul + bloody discharge → Foreign body, age 3–9 yr  |  Unexplained bleeding, especially <6 yr → Expedited vaginoscopy
smell, color, and the child's age do most of the diagnostic work
Nonspecific vulvovaginitis accounts for roughly 80%-90% of pediatric gynecology visits because the prepubertal vagina is unestrogenized, thin, and has a neutral-to-slightly-alkaline pH — a poor local defense that makes irritation, poor hygiene, and minor infection the default explanation for discharge. First-line management is simply hygiene measures and warm-water sitz baths for about two weeks; only persistent symptoms warrant investigating a specific pathogen, a foreign body, an underlying skin disease, or abuse. A foreign body (toilet tissue is the classic culprit, typically age 3-9 years, accounting for roughly 4% of visits) announces itself with foul-smelling, blood-tinged discharge rather than plain discharge alone. Bleeding without discharge is its own category: unexplained vaginal bleeding, especially in a child under 6 years who is not yet estrogenized, should prompt expedited vaginoscopy rather than watchful waiting, since the differential includes foreign body, tumor, or trauma.
MRKH: Definition and the Dilation-First Ladder
46,XX + normal puberty (functioning ovaries) + absent/short vagina + absent or rudimentary uterus  →  FIRST-LINE: progressive vaginal dilation 15–20 min/day for 3–6 mo or longer  →  surgical neovagina only if dilation fails or is unacceptable
normal puberty plus absent periods plus absent/short vagina = think MRKH, not an endocrine problem
Mayer-Rokitansky-Kuster-Hauser syndrome affects roughly 1 in 5,000 people with a 46,XX karyotype whose ovaries function normally, so puberty (breast development, growth) proceeds on schedule while the uterus and upper vagina fail to form — the presentation is primary amenorrhea with otherwise normal secondary sexual characteristics. About 75% of patients have complete vaginal agenesis and 25% have only a short vaginal pouch; associated anomalies are common, with urinary tract anomalies in up to about half of patients and skeletal anomalies in roughly 12%. Management always starts conservative: progressive vaginal dilation for 15-20 minutes a day, continued for 3-6 months or longer, creates a functional vagina in most patients and is first-line before any surgery is considered; a surgical neovagina is reserved for dilation failure or an unacceptable result. Because the uterus is absent or nonfunctional, intrauterine gestation is not possible even though the ovaries can still be used for oocyte retrieval and gestational-carrier pregnancy.
Topical Therapy Ladders for Prepubertal Vulvar Disease
Labial adhesion, symptomatic → topical estrogen BID ×2–8 wk (avoid beyond ~6–8 wk) → topical corticosteroid BID ×6–8 wk if persistent  |  Urethral prolapse → conservative care + topical estrogen  |  Pediatric lichen sclerosus (white figure-8) → high-potency topical steroid 4–6 wk, then taper, monitor q6–12 mo
asymptomatic disease is observed; symptomatic disease gets a fixed-duration topical course, never indefinite treatment
Three prepubertal vulvar conditions share the same logic: observe if asymptomatic, treat with a time-limited topical course if symptomatic, and escalate only if that course fails. Labial adhesions are most common between ages 2 and 6 years (up to 90% occur before age 6); asymptomatic fusion is simply observed, while symptomatic adhesions get topical estrogen twice daily for 2-8 weeks — prolonged use beyond about 6-8 weeks is avoided — and a topical corticosteroid twice daily for 6-8 weeks is the next step if estrogen alone does not resolve it. Recurrence after separation is common (25%-65%), so a barrier ointment such as petrolatum or zinc oxide is continued for at least a month afterward. Urethral prolapse presents as a circumferential red 'donut' of tissue around the meatus and responds to conservative care plus topical estrogen, with surgery reserved for a persistent or necrotic prolapse. Pediatric lichen sclerosus shows the classic white, figure-eight distribution around the vulva and perianal area; it is treated with a high-potency topical corticosteroid for an initial 4-6 week interval (a midpotency taper over roughly 2-3 weeks, or hydrocortisone tapered over about 2 weeks, may follow), with biopsy reserved for an uncertain or refractory diagnosis and clinical monitoring every 6-12 months thereafter.
Pediatric Ovarian and Vaginal Tumors: Marker, Stage, Preserve
Adnexal mass → about 75%–85% benign / 15%–25% malignant  |  Dysgerminoma → LDH (10%–15% bilateral)  |  Yolk-sac tumor → AFP  |  Grape-like vaginal mass + bleeding, usually <6 yr → sarcoma botryoides (VAC + surgery, NOT FIGO-staged)  |  Stage IA pure dysgerminoma or grade-1 immature teratoma → surgery alone may suffice
preserve the uterus and the normal contralateral ovary whenever it is oncologically safe to do so
Ovarian neoplasms make up only about 1% of premenarcheal tumors, but when a pediatric ovarian mass does require surgery, roughly 75%-85% turn out benign and only 15%-25% malignant — a favorable ratio that supports a fertility-preserving surgical approach whenever it is oncologically safe. Two tumor-marker pairings are classic: dysgerminoma tracks with LDH and has a 10%-15% rate of bilateral involvement, while yolk-sac (endodermal sinus) tumor tracks with AFP. FIGO ovarian staging (I confined to the ovary/ovaries, II pelvic extension, III abdominal/retroperitoneal nodal disease, IV distant metastasis) still applies to these germ-cell tumors, and a genuinely favorable subset — Stage IA pure dysgerminoma or grade-1 immature teratoma — may be managed with surgery alone, without chemotherapy. Sarcoma botryoides is a different disease entirely: a grape-like vaginal mass causing bleeding, usually appearing before age 6 and rarely after age 8, treated with combination chemotherapy (VAC) plus surgery through a multidisciplinary pediatric oncology team — and because it arises from the vagina rather than the ovary, it is explicitly not staged using the ovarian FIGO system.

🏁Final 30-Second PLE Recall

  • Active-phase arrest (Labor) needs >=6cm + ROM + no cervical change despite 4h of adequate contractions (>200 MVU) or 6h of inadequate contractions plus oxytocin -- the old 2-hour rule is outdated.
  • A positive aneuploidy screen (Prenatal Care: cfDNA, quad screen, NT/PAPP-A) only earns a diagnostic test (CVS 9-12wk or amniocentesis >15wk) -- it is never itself the diagnosis.
  • Placenta previa plus a prior cesarean (Hemorrhagic Complications) is placenta accreta spectrum until excluded -- never pull on an adherent placenta; leave it in situ and proceed to planned hysterectomy.
  • Methotrexate eligibility for ectopic pregnancy (Early Pregnancy Risk) requires hCG <5,000, mass generally <5cm, and no fetal cardiac activity; success is judged by a >=15% hCG decline from day 4 to day 7.
  • Macrosomia cesarean-offer thresholds differ by diabetes status (Fetal Complications): EFW >4,500g if diabetic, >5,000g if not.
  • Severe blood pressure (Hypertension: >=160/110) is treated within minutes -- it never waits for the usual 4-hour repeat-confirmation rule used to diagnose ordinary gestational hypertension.
  • GDM diagnosis (Diabetes During Pregnancy) needs >=2 abnormal values on the 100-g, 3-hour OGTT (cutoffs 95/180/155/140 mg/dL) -- a single abnormal value is not diagnostic.
  • A new seizure at >=20 weeks with hypertension or proteinuria (Seizures & Medical Illnesses) is treated as eclampsia with magnesium sulfate, not a routine antiepileptic, regardless of any prior seizure history.
  • In Surgical Disorders During Pregnancy, pregnancy never delays a clearly indicated operation -- treat complicated disease now and pick the approach that minimizes fetal harm, not the option of no treatment.
  • Only two perinatal infections actually change delivery route: active HSV lesions/prodrome at labor onset (cesarean) and HIV RNA >1,000 copies/mL or unknown near delivery (scheduled cesarean at 38 wk) -- GBS, syphilis, gonorrhea/chlamydia, and HBV are all treated and delivered by usual obstetric indications.
  • Postpartum endometritis (5-10 days postpartum, fever plus uterine tenderness +/- foul lochia) is treated with IV clindamycin plus gentamicin until afebrile 24-48h; mastitis is treated with dicloxacillin while breastfeeding/pumping continues.
  • The methylergonovine-avoid-in-HTN/preeclampsia vs. carboprost-avoid-in-asthma contraindication pairing repeats identically across Labor, Hemorrhage, Hypertension, Seizures/Medical Illness, and Postpartum Care -- memorize the pairing, not just the drug names.
  • The magnesium sulfate card is one unit everywhere it appears: 6g IV load over 15-20min, then 2g/h maintenance, an extra 2g IV over 3-5min for a recurrent seizure, and IV calcium gluconate as the toxicity antidote.
  • Instability always overrides a gestational-age or numeric algorithm -- an unstable, hemorrhaging, or actively seizing patient is stabilized/delivered/operated on now, regardless of what week-based rule would otherwise apply.
  • Tocolysis (seen across Labor, Fetal Complications, Hemorrhage, and Seizures/Medical Illness) exists only to buy ~48 hours for corticosteroids or transfer -- it is withheld whenever there is a competing indication for immediate delivery, such as infection, abruption, or fetal compromise.
  • Topic L site map: Nabothian = cervix, Gartner = lateral vaginal wall, Skene = periurethral, Bartholin = posterolateral introitus — location alone usually names the cyst.
  • Topic M: FIGO fibroid types 0-2 touch the cavity (bleeding/infertility, hysteroscopic removal); types 3-7 spare it (bulk symptoms, myomectomy or hysterectomy depending on fertility desire).
  • Topic N: CHC's MEC-4 list clusters around vascular risk — migraine WITH aura, BP ≥160/100, VTE/CAD/stroke history, hepatic adenoma, heavy smoking after 35; progestin-only methods and IUDs almost never hit MEC 4 for these same conditions.
  • Topic O: every stage of syphilis gets penicillin — only the schedule/route changes (single IM dose early, weekly IM x3 late latent/unknown, prolonged IV for neurosyphilis).
  • Topic P: POP-Q stages a prolapse purely as distance from the hymen — Stage II means within 1 cm of the hymen in EITHER direction, not sitting exactly at it.
  • Topic Q: menopause is a clinical diagnosis (12 months of amenorrhea, average age 51); any patient with an intact uterus on systemic estrogen must also get a progestin.
  • Topic R: parametrial invasion is the Stage IIB line in cervical cancer — cross it and management shifts from surgery to cisplatin-based chemoradiation.
  • Topic S: PALM-COEIN sorts AUB into structural (Polyp/Adenomyosis/Leiomyoma/Malignancy) versus nonstructural (Coagulopathy/Ovulatory/Endometrial/Iatrogenic/Not-yet-classified) causes — always after a beta-hCG.
  • Topic T: the preeclampsia ladder resets the delivery clock at each rung — no severe features to 37 weeks, severe features to 34 weeks if stable, eclampsia delivers once mother/seizure are stabilized regardless of gestational age.
  • Topic U: MRKH is 46,XX with functioning ovaries (normal puberty) but an absent/short vagina and absent/rudimentary uterus; first-line treatment is vaginal dilation, not hormones or immediate surgery.
  • Endometrial hyperplasia risk barely moves with architecture alone (simple 1% to complex 3%) but jumps sharply the moment atypia appears (8% to 29%, EIN 27-52%) — this exact ladder is taught in both Topic M and Topic S.
  • Two similarly-numbered 'WHO' scales are NOT the same thing: WHO Groups I-IV (Topic Q) sort anovulatory infertility by FSH/estrogen pattern, while WHO/US MEC categories 1-4 (Topic N) grade a contraceptive's safety in a given patient.
  • One germ-cell marker pairing holds at any age: LDH names dysgerminoma, AFP names yolk-sac tumor — tested identically in adult gyn-onc (Topic R) and pediatric gyn (Topic U).
  • Age-based biopsy triggers differ by organ: a new Bartholin-area mass at age >40 (Topic L) and AUB at age ≥45 (Topics Q/S) both bypass routine reassurance for tissue diagnosis, but the cutoff age itself is different — don't merge them.
